**Re:** Copyright and Fair-Use Basis for the DeSciX/EGPT **EGPT Digital Library**

### Question Presented

Whether U.S. copyright law permits a noncommercial, public-access digital research library—implemented through the public **DeSciX/EGPT GitHub repository**—to lawfully acquire books, papers, photographs, correspondence, obituaries, memoirs, and related historical materials; digitize those materials; perform OCR and full-text indexing; construct bibliographic and provenance records; cross-reference persons, events, documents, and ideas; expose search results; quote relevant passages; and reproduce historically significant pages or artifacts when necessary to explain their historical significance.

The contemplated library would use materials concerning figures including Albert Einstein, John von Neumann, Kurt Gödel, Stanislaw Ulam, Gian-Carlo Rota and their contemporaries. By way of example, it could connect von Neumann correspondence with Gödel correspondence, Ulam's writings and obituary materials, *Adventures of a Mathematician*, institutional records, photographs, and contemporaneous publications in order to reconstruct historically significant threads in the development of mathematics, computation, physics, and government-sponsored scientific research.

This memorandum does **not** attempt to establish numerical limits on permissible quotation, page reproduction, or public display. Those questions depend upon the particular work and particular use and are reserved.

### Brief Answer

There is substantial federal appellate authority supporting the central architecture of the proposed library under **17 U.S.C. § 107 fair use**, provided the copyrighted materials are being used for the distinct purposes of historical scholarship, research, search, documentation, provenance, and analysis rather than simply made available as substitutes for the original books or other works.

Section 107 expressly identifies “criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching … scholarship, or research” as paradigmatic fair-use purposes. It requires consideration of four factors: purpose and character, nature of the copyrighted work, amount and substantiality used, and market effect. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="0"}

Several appellate cases map unusually closely onto different components of the proposed system:

- ***Authors Guild, Inc. v. HathiTrust*, 755 F.3d 87 (2d Cir. 2014)** upheld digitization of entire copyrighted books to create a full-text searchable research database. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="1"}
- ***Authors Guild v. Google, Inc.*, 804 F.3d 202 (2d Cir. 2015)** upheld the scanning, machine-reading, indexing, searching, and limited display of copyrighted books, even though Google copied the books in their entirety without individual copyright-owner permission. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="2"}
- ***Bill Graham Archives v. Dorling Kindersley Ltd.*, 448 F.3d 605 (2d Cir. 2006)** upheld reproduction of entire copyrighted visual works because the works were being used as historical artifacts within a chronological historical account rather than for their original expressive purpose. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="3"}
- ***Sundeman v. Seajay Society, Inc.*, 142 F.3d 194 (4th Cir. 1998)** upheld substantial copying associated with scholarly examination of an unpublished literary manuscript, demonstrating the breadth fair use can have in bona fide historical and scholarly research. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="4"}
- ***Núñez v. Caribbean International News Corp.*, 235 F.3d 18 (1st Cir. 2000)** upheld reproduction of entire copyrighted photographs where the photographs themselves had become relevant objects of public reporting and discussion. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="5"}

The principal contrary boundary is ***Hachette Book Group, Inc. v. Internet Archive*, 115 F.4th 163 (2d Cir. 2024)**. There, the Second Circuit rejected fair use where Internet Archive digitized copyrighted print books and made the resulting ebooks available to users to read in full. The critical distinction is that the resulting digital books performed substantially the **same function as ordinary ebooks** and could substitute for licensed electronic copies. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="6"}

Indeed, the district court in *Hachette* expressly noted that the publishers were **not challenging Internet Archive's indexing, searching, short excerpts, citations, or text-and-data-mining research uses**. The litigation concerned its distribution of readable electronic versions of entire books. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="7"}

Accordingly, the strongest legal characterization of the proposed DeSciX/EGPT library is not “an online lending library.” It is a **historical research and provenance system whose copyrighted source materials are inputs and evidentiary historical objects used to create search, historical relationships, documentation, commentary, and new knowledge**.

That characterization fits considerably more closely with *HathiTrust*, *Google Books*, *Bill Graham Archives*, *Sundeman*, and *Núñez* than with *Hachette*.

# Governing Law

## I. Section 107 Fair Use Is the Primary Doctrine

The central statutory provision is **17 U.S.C. § 107**.

Congress expressly provides that fair use of a copyrighted work for purposes including:

> “criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching … scholarship, or research”

is not copyright infringement. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="8"}

The statute identifies four factors:

1. the purpose and character of the challenged use, including its commercial or nonprofit educational nature;
2. the nature of the copyrighted work;
3. the amount and substantiality of the material used; and
4. the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="9"}

No factor is independently dispositive, and fair use remains a contextual inquiry.

The proposed historical system implicates §107 directly because **scholarship and research are expressly identified in the statute**.

## II. Section 108 Provides Additional Rights to Qualifying Libraries and Archives but Does Not Displace Fair Use

Section 108 separately permits qualifying libraries and archives to make certain reproductions under specified conditions. Among its threshold requirements, reproduction generally must lack a purpose of direct or indirect commercial advantage; the collection must be open to the public or available to unaffiliated specialized researchers; and copies must contain appropriate copyright notices. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="10"}

But §108 is **not the ceiling on what a library can do**.

The legislative history accompanying §107 expressly states that fair use continues to apply to libraries and that nothing in §108 takes away rights otherwise existing under fair use. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="11"}

Consequently, even if DeSciX/EGPT qualifies independently as a library or archive under §108, the broader and more important doctrinal basis for the research architecture described here remains **§107**.

# Analysis

## I. Full Digitization, OCR and Indexing for Historical Search Closely Track *HathiTrust* and *Google Books*

The strongest precedent for the system's technical architecture is *Authors Guild v. HathiTrust*.

Participating libraries possessed millions of books that had been digitally scanned. The resulting HathiTrust Digital Library permitted searching across the full text of the books. The Second Circuit held that fair use permitted creation of a **full-text searchable database of copyrighted works**. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="12"}

The significance is substantial. Fair use was not confined to copying isolated sentences or pages. Creation of the search system necessarily required digital reproduction and machine processing of **entire works**.

The public-facing purpose was nevertheless materially different from the purpose for which the books had originally been published. A book exists to communicate its expressive contents to a reader; the search database used the book's contents as data against which queries could be executed.

*Google Books* extended the principle.

Google scanned and retained digital copies of tens of millions of books contributed by libraries, made them machine-readable, indexed their contents, enabled public full-text searching, and returned responsive snippets. The Second Circuit affirmed fair use. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="13"}

The proposed DeSciX/EGPT architecture has the same foundational operations:

**source document → digitization → OCR/machine-readable representation → indexing → search → research result.**

But it adds another layer of scholarly transformation:

**search results → provenance relationships → chronological and intellectual cross-referencing → reconstruction of historical developments.**

Thus the proposed library does not merely ask whether the phrase “stored program” appears in a book. It could establish that:

- document A contains a statement by von Neumann;
- document B records a related statement from Gödel;
- document C dates Ulam's involvement in an institution;
- document D provides contemporaneous institutional evidence;
- document E later recounts the event autobiographically; and
- those independent evidentiary sources establish or contest a particular historical chronology.

That function is substantially different from the function of the source books themselves.

## II. *Bill Graham Archives* Supports Treating Copyrighted Works as Historical Evidence

*Bill Graham Archives v. Dorling Kindersley* is especially important for the portion of the project that displays historically significant source artifacts.

The case concerned copyrighted concert posters and tickets reproduced in a historical book about the Grateful Dead. The Second Circuit affirmed fair use. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="14"}

The essential distinction was functional. The original works had been created as promotional or artistic materials. In the later historical work, they functioned as **documentary evidence within a historical chronology**.

That distinction maps directly onto the proposed archive.

Consider a letter from von Neumann to Gödel.

As originally written, its immediate function was interpersonal correspondence.

Within the proposed system, the same document could function as:

- evidence establishing that communication occurred;
- evidence fixing a particular date;
- evidence that one participant knew of another's work;
- evidence identifying terminology used at the time;
- evidence connecting two otherwise independent documentary collections; or
- an artifact through which a contested historical proposition can be independently evaluated.

Similarly, the visual appearance of a manuscript page, annotation, photograph, telegram, typescript, or marked-up document may itself carry evidentiary significance.

Under the reasoning of *Bill Graham Archives*, reproduction undertaken for that historical documentary function is meaningfully different from exploiting the original work for the purpose for which it was originally created.

## III. *Sundeman* Supports Scholarly Processing of Historically Important Manuscript Material

*Sundeman v. Seajay Society* concerned an unpublished manuscript by Marjorie Kinnan Rawlings.

The Fourth Circuit affirmed fair use in circumstances involving reproduction undertaken to facilitate scholarly analysis and presentation. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="15"}

The case is important for two reasons.

First, it demonstrates that unpublished status does not automatically defeat fair use. Congress itself subsequently made explicit that:

> “The fact that a work is unpublished shall not itself bar a finding of fair use”

when the statutory factors otherwise support it. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="16"}

Second, *Sundeman* recognizes the legitimate necessity of copying involved in serious scholarship surrounding primary-source materials.

That principle matters for collections of historical correspondence, working papers, drafts, manuscripts, and other materials that may never have been conventionally published.

The proposed archive's treatment of such materials is not simply republication. Its purpose is to make the materials intelligible within a researched historical record.

## IV. *Núñez* Supports Reproduction Where the Copyrighted Object Itself Is Historically Significant

*Núñez v. Caribbean International News* involved copyrighted photographs that themselves became objects of a public controversy. The First Circuit upheld their reproduction under fair use. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="17"}

That case reinforces an important conceptual distinction.

Sometimes a copyrighted object is not simply a source from which information is taken. **The object itself is part of the event being documented.**

That can readily occur in the history of mathematics and computing.

Examples could include:

- a handwritten mathematical note whose notation is historically relevant;
- a marked-up manuscript demonstrating changes between drafts;
- a photograph documenting who attended a meeting;
- a letter whose letterhead establishes institutional affiliation;
- handwritten marginalia;
- diagrams;
- telegrams;
- typescript corrections; or
- contemporaneous reproductions whose physical or visual attributes contribute to provenance.

In those circumstances, describing the artifact without showing it can destroy some of the historical information that the scholarly work seeks to convey.

*Núñez* and *Bill Graham Archives* together provide significant precedent for considering that difference in the fair-use analysis.

# V. The Combination of Sources Produces a Research Purpose Distinct From Any Individual Source Work

The proposed library has an additional characteristic not captured fully by any single case.

The historical object being created is not merely a digital copy of one source.

It is a **network of provenance**.

For example:

**von Neumann letter**  
↕  
**Gödel correspondence**  
↕  
**institutional chronology**  
↕  
**Ulam memoir**  
↕  
**Ulam obituary or memorial account**  
↕  
**contemporaneous publication**  
↕  
**government or university records**

The resulting scholarly object answers questions that no individual copyrighted source necessarily answers.

The system can expose:

- chronology;
- corroboration;
- contradiction;
- chains of citation;
- personal and institutional overlap;
- evolution of terminology;
- development of mathematical ideas;
- movement of researchers among institutions;
- changes between contemporaneous records and retrospective recollections; and
- provenance underlying historical claims.

Copyright protects the expression contained within individual source works. It does not grant ownership over historical facts, relationships, chronology, ideas, methods, or discoveries merely because evidence concerning them appears in copyrighted works.

The proposed library therefore uses copyrighted sources as **evidence from which a separately constructed historical account can be investigated and verified**.

That function strongly resembles the scholarly, analytic, and information-location functions recognized in the foregoing cases.

# VI. *Warhol v. Goldsmith* Requires Attention to the Particular Challenged Use, but Does Not Undermine the Search-and-Scholarship Cases

The Supreme Court's most important recent fair-use decision is ***Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S. 508 (2023)**.

The Court emphasized that “transformative” cannot mean merely that a later user has added new expression or meaning. Courts must examine the **specific challenged use** and determine whether its purpose and character are sufficiently different from the original use. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="18"}

That requirement makes precise characterization of DeSciX/EGPT important.

The relevant uses are not abstractly “putting books on the Internet.”

They are:

- machine-readable indexing;
- historical search;
- provenance establishment;
- cross-document correlation;
- scholarly commentary;
- reconstruction of chronology;
- source verification; and
- presentation of historically significant artifacts as evidence.

Those uses have independently identifiable purposes.

*Warhol* itself reaffirmed that criticism, comment, scholarship, and research are statutory examples of uses that characteristically employ an original work for a purpose different from simply supplying the original expression to its customary audience. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="19"}

Thus *Warhol* makes the **purpose definition more important**, rather than displacing *Google Books*, *HathiTrust*, or historical-documentation precedent.

---

# VII. *Hachette v. Internet Archive* Defines an Important Boundary and, Properly Understood, Helps Distinguish This Project

The most important recent adverse precedent for digital libraries is *Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive*.

Internet Archive acquired physical books, digitized them, and allowed users to borrow and read complete digital editions. Outside its temporary National Emergency Library program, Internet Archive generally limited simultaneous digital circulation according to the number of corresponding physical copies it possessed.

The Second Circuit nevertheless rejected the fair-use defense. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="20"}

The critical problem was purpose.

The digitized copy remained fundamentally a **book offered for reading as a book**.

It consequently competed with the ordinary market for publisher-authorized ebooks.

That is not the contemplated function of DeSciX/EGPT.

Indeed, the district court's opinion in *Hachette* is unusually useful in drawing the line. The publishers expressly did **not** challenge Internet Archive's:

- indexing for searching;
- display of short excerpts in response to searches;
- citations; or
- research involving text and data mining.

Their claims targeted the public digital lending of complete readable ebook versions. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="21"}

That distinction is nearly a direct description of the line relevant here.

The proposed **EGPT Digital Library** is built around the uses that were **not the subject of the publishers' infringement claims in Hachette**, while avoiding reliance upon the proposition rejected there—that ownership of a print book alone permits creation and public lending of a substitute digital edition.

# VIII. Noncommercial and Public Scholarly Operation Strengthens, but Does Not Determine, the Analysis

Section 107 specifically instructs courts to consider whether a use is commercial or nonprofit educational. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="22"}

The proposed facts therefore carry additional weight:

- no subscription fee;
- no sale of substituted digital editions;
- no advertising exploitation of the materials;
- no commercial licensing business;
- an openly documented research architecture;
- public scholarly access;
- bibliographic documentation;
- provenance;
- research-oriented search; and
- preservation and historical analysis.

Noncommercial status does not automatically produce fair use. Likewise, commercial status does not automatically defeat it—Google itself was a commercial enterprise in *Google Books*. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="23"}

But where a project's actual function is scholarship and historical research, its noncommercial structure provides additional evidence that the asserted research purpose is genuine rather than a pretext for substitution.

# IX. Section 108 Could Provide an Additional Library/Archive Layer

If organized as a qualifying library or archive, the project could potentially invoke particular protections under §108 in addition to §107.

Section 108 contemplates organizations whose collections are open to the public or to outside researchers in specialized fields and whose copying occurs without direct or indirect commercial advantage. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="24"}

The proposed project's:

- public access;
- noncommercial structure;
- organized historical collection;
- provenance system;
- research function; and
- collection of specialized mathematical and computational history

are structurally consistent with those statutory characteristics.

But the legal analysis need not depend upon establishing §108 status.

Congress expressly preserved fair use independently of §108. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="25"}

Thus the better architecture is:

**§107 = principal authority for research, scholarship, historical analysis, search, and contextual reproduction**

while

**§108 = possible additional library/archive privileges where its particular statutory conditions are satisfied.**

# X. Overall Application to the Proposed DeSciX/EGPT Architecture

The significant legal question is not whether copyrighted materials enter the database. *Google Books* and *HathiTrust* establish that complete copyrighted works may, under appropriate circumstances, be copied into digital systems for lawful search and research purposes. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="26"}

Nor is the decisive question whether an entire historical artifact is ever reproduced. *Bill Graham Archives* and *Núñez* demonstrate that reproduction of complete copyrighted visual works can constitute fair use where the objects themselves perform a documentary or historical function. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="27"}

Nor does unpublished status categorically preclude historical scholarship. *Sundeman* and the text of §107 demonstrate otherwise. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="28"}

The principal doctrinal question is instead:

**What is the copyrighted material being used to accomplish?**

For DeSciX/EGPT, the stated answer is:

> to establish provenance, permit historical search, correlate independent sources, reconstruct chronology, permit verification of historical propositions, identify relationships among people and institutions, and explain historically significant artifacts.

That is a research and historical-documentation function substantially distinct from ordinary consumption of the underlying works.

By contrast:

> making *Adventures of a Mathematician* available so that a member of the public can read the complete copyrighted book instead of purchasing or borrowing an authorized edition

would implicate the substantially different *Hachette* fact pattern.

That distinction should be maintained architecturally as well as rhetorically.

# Risk Considerations

The principal legal risks do not arise from the existence of a digital research corpus itself. They arise when particular public-facing uses begin to resemble market substitutes for copyrighted originals.

Accordingly, the project's strongest posture results from maintaining a demonstrable relationship between each public-facing reproduction and a scholarly function: search, historical proposition, commentary, provenance, comparison, chronology, criticism, documentation, or presentation of the artifact itself as historical evidence.

The project's public GitHub implementation is potentially useful in this respect because its source code, data structures, and documentation can provide objective evidence of what the system actually does.

For example, an architecture visibly structured around entities such as:

**Person → Document → Date → Institution → Event → Claim → Citation → Provenance**

supports the asserted historical-research function more directly than an architecture whose primary objects are:

**Book → Download → Reader.**

Fair use turns upon actual use rather than labeling. *Warhol* makes this particularly clear. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="29"}

Accordingly, calling the system a “library” is legally less important under §107 than ensuring that its actual operation reflects the research purposes described above.

# Conclusion

Existing federal appellate precedent provides substantial support for the proposed **EGPT Digital Library**.

Taken together, the cases establish several propositions directly relevant to the proposed DeSciX/EGPT architecture:

**First**, copyrighted books may be digitized in their entirety when complete digitization is necessary to create full-text search and research functionality. *HathiTrust*; *Google Books*. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="30"}

**Second**, copyrighted works may themselves be reproduced as historical artifacts where their documentary significance is part of the historical account being created. *Bill Graham Archives*. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="31"}

**Third**, scholarly treatment of unpublished historical materials can qualify as fair use. *Sundeman*. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="32"}

**Fourth**, complete copyrighted images may sometimes be reproduced where the images themselves constitute the subject or evidence of the event being reported or analyzed. *Núñez*. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="33"}

**Fifth**, a digital archive cannot rely merely upon acquisition of a physical book and nonprofit status to provide the public with a substitute readable ebook. *Hachette*. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="34"}

And notably, the *Hachette* record distinguishes exactly the research functions contemplated here: the publishers did not challenge Internet Archive's indexing, searching, citations, responsive excerpts, and text/data-mining functions. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="35"}

The proposed project therefore sits on a materially different doctrinal footing from a generalized digital lending service.

Its defining activity is not the substitution of a digitized copyrighted work for the original. It is the use of lawfully acquired historical sources to construct a **searchable, cross-referenced, provenance-based historical record**—one in which a von Neumann letter can be connected to a Gödel letter, Ulam's contemporaneous or retrospective account, institutional records, publications, photographs, and other independent evidence so that historical claims can be researched, reconstructed, challenged, and verified.

That activity falls squarely within the statutory purposes of **scholarship and research**, and important components of its technical implementation have already been upheld in closely analogous federal appellate decisions.

The separate question of precisely how much of any particular copyrighted source may appropriately be displayed to the public should be addressed at the level of the individual source and use. It is not necessary to resolve that question in order to conclude that the **underlying historical-search, digitization, OCR, indexing, provenance, cross-referencing, and documentary architecture itself has substantial support in existing fair-use precedent.**
