# An idiot, or natural fool: Blackstone's definition

<!-- Filed 30 September 2026 by the assistant on the author's instruction to seek the source behind the common-law definition quoted in guide/avert-the-singularity.md; his pass. -->

The English common law's definition of idiocy, as William Blackstone states it in the
*Commentaries on the Laws of England*, Book I, chapter 8 (the king's ordinary revenue, the
custody of idiots and lunatics), 3rd edition, 1768, vol. I, p. 302:

> An idiot, or natural fool, is one that hath had no understanding from his nativity; and
> therefore is by law presumed never likely to attain any.

As printed, with the long s of the 1768 setting:

> An idiot, or natural fool, is one that hath had no underſtanding from his nativity; and
> therefore is by law preſumed never likely to attain any.

The older test, Fitzherbert's in the *New Natura Brevium* (sixteenth century), as quoted in
the Yale Law Journal note "A Reassessment of Common Law Protections for 'Idiots'"
(https://yalelawjournal.org/note/a-reassessment-of-common-law-protections-for-idiots, notes
123 and 125):

> such a person who cannot account or number twenty pence, nor can tell who was his father or
> mother, nor how old he is, etc., so as it may appear that he hath no understanding of reason
> what shall be for his profit, or what for his loss.

The guide's line, in `guide/avert-the-singularity.md`, gives the definition as "he who hath no
capacity for understanding"; Blackstone's words are "one that hath had no understanding from
his nativity".
