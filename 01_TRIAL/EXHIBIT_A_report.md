# Los Alamos Computation Artificial Intelligence and Gian-Carlo Rota

*An evidence graded historical assessment under a more probable than not standard*

**Research report**

Prepared 12 September 2026

Scope  Manhattan Project and thermonuclear computation through Rota’s later Los Alamos and United States government activity


## Who von Neumann was, by the end

He is remembered as a prodigy: the architect of the stored program computer, the founder of game theory, the Hungarian who could do arithmetic in his head faster than the machines he designed, one of the physicists at Los Alamos. That is the public picture, and it stops around 1950. What the public record shows, and what is not generally known, is what he had become by the end of his life. He was not a distinguished scientist whom the government consulted. He was the man who set the direction of the highest priority research programs of the United States.

The offices are documented and they are not ceremonial. In October 1954 President Eisenhower appointed him a Commissioner of the Atomic Energy Commission, one of five men with statutory authority over the entire American nuclear enterprise and its Restricted Data. He chaired the Strategic Missiles Evaluation Committee, whose report launched the American intercontinental ballistic missile program. At the 258th meeting of the National Security Council the Vice President asked him directly whether the missile directive went far enough; his amendment was adopted at the table, and the ICBM was assigned the highest national priority. He held standing positions across the weapons, missile and computing programs for seventeen years. He received the Medal of Freedom, and the Atomic Energy Commission’s first Fermi Award. When he was dying at Walter Reed, the Secretary of Defense, the Deputy Secretary, the Secretaries of the three services and the Joint Chiefs assembled around his bed so that the work could continue.

Ulam supplies the context in the official memorial, and it is the piece usually passed over. Writing in the Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society in 1958, Ulam records what von Neumann did with the Commission seat: among other things, the organization of studies of mathematical computing machines. The man with statutory authority over the nuclear program was using it, in part, to organize the study of computation. And in the same memorial Ulam states what von Neumann was building with the rest of his attention: “it was toward the end of his life that he felt sure enough of himself to engage freely and yet painstakingly in the creation of a possible new mathematical discipline. This was to be a combinatorial theory of automata and organisms.”


## He had heard the alternatives and chose against them

Von Neumann was not working in isolation from the ideas that would define the rest of the century. He was in the room for them.

On 23 and 24 March 1950, at the Seventh Conference on Cybernetics in New York, Claude Shannon presented “The Redundancy of English.” Shannon attended as a guest; von Neumann is listed among the members and, unlike five others that year, carries no absence mark. At that same meeting, in the discussion of Ralph Gerard’s paper on digital notions in the central nervous system, von Neumann entered a caveat of his own: “It is very plausible, indeed, that the underlying mechanism of the nervous system may be best, although somewhat loosely, described as an analogical mechanism.” And on the question of coding: “It seems to me that we do not know at this moment to what extent coded messages are used in the nervous system … whether any of the coded ways in which messages are sent operate in any manner similar to our digital system. If I understand the evidence correctly, it is nonexistent in this regard.” The transcript does not record him speaking in Shannon’s session. It records him, at the same conference, refusing the reading of the nervous system that the information theoretic program would go on to assume.

He had already announced the methodological position in print. “The Mathematician,” published in 1947, is not a survey. It is a declaration about where mathematics goes wrong: “As a mathematical discipline travels far from its empirical source … it is beset with very grave dangers. It becomes more and more purely aestheticizing, more and more purely l’art pour l’art … At the inception the style is usually classical; when it shows signs of becoming baroque, then the danger signal is up.” And the remedy: “the rejuvenating return to the source: the re-injection of more or less directly empirical ideas.” In the same essay he notes that Brouwer’s reconstruction would have invalidated “a good fifty per cent of modern mathematics, in its most vital — and up to then unquestioned — parts,” and treats the foundations as genuinely unsettled rather than settled by convention.

So the record shows a man who knew the available routes — the information theoretic, the quantum formal, the discrete and automata theoretic — who said in public which way he thought mathematics and physics had to go, who chose differently from the academic consensus in both fields, and who then spent his last seven years in the offices that decide what the United States government funds and classifies.


## Then he died, and the question this report asks

He died on 8 February 1957, at fifty three. Nothing of the new science was in print. The Hixon lecture of 1948 contained no cellular automaton. The 1949 Illinois lectures he never wrote up. The automata manuscript was a first draft abandoned in 1953, and Arthur Burks would take nine years to correct it, name its parts, supply most of its figures and write a final chapter of his own before it could be published at all. The Computer and the Brain was unfinished on his desk. What existed was a programme held in one man’s head, in an office nobody else held, under a clearance almost nobody else had.

The circle scattered along visible lines. Fermi had died in 1954. Feynman had already gone — out of the weapons world to Cornell and then Caltech, into open academic physics, where three decades later he would take the other route in public and become a founder of quantum computing. Ulam and Metropolis stayed inside, kept building on laboratory machines, and wrote books rather than papers about what they were doing. Teller stayed in weapons.

Von Neumann personally held three separate things, and they are worth separating before asking what became of them. He held the science — the theory of automata and organisms, and the discrete and stochastic direction it belonged to. He held the machines and the computational practice built around them. And he held governmental authority, the Commission seat and the chairmanships. One man had all three. On 8 February 1957 all three came loose at once.

What the record shows happening to each is the beginning of the answer.


## What became of the three things he held

The authority passed to someone outside science. His seat on the Atomic Energy Commission stood vacant for seven months. On 12 September 1957 Eisenhower appointed John Stephens Graham, a lawyer and a Democrat, in what was described at the time as a bipartisan gesture; Graham served until 1962. Ulam’s own list in the memorial records what else von Neumann had been holding — chairman of the Advisory Committee on Guided Missiles from 1954, the Aberdeen Ballistic Research Laboratory scientific advisory committee from 1940, the Air Force Scientific Advisory Board from 1951, the Technical Advisory Panel on Atomic Energy from 1953 — and notes that when he took the Commission seat he “discontinued all commitments with the exception of the chairmanship of the ICBM Committee.”

The machines were dismantled. The Institute for Advanced Study Electronic Computer Project, the machine von Neumann built and the design copied around the world, did not survive him by eighteen months. The decision that no new machine would be built there had been taken by early 1956; responsibility for the existing one transferred to Princeton University on 1 July 1957; and at midnight on 15 July 1958 Julian Bigelow disconnected the power. Princeton’s stated reason was that the machine was “essentially developmental and not very carefully engineered.” The university declined to keep it running once the maintenance estimate rose from ten thousand dollars a year to about a hundred and ten thousand, bought an IBM 650 instead, and the machine went to the Smithsonian in 1960. The staff scattered: Goldstine and Pomerene to IBM, Slutz to the Bureau of Standards, Ware to RAND, Charney to MIT. Only Bigelow remained, his membership permanent and unterminable, his salary frozen. Freeman Dyson’s account of it is blunt: “When von Neumann tragically died, the snobs took their revenge and got rid of the computing project root and branch.”

And the science was in nobody’s hands, because a large part of it had never been written down. The most direct statement of this comes from Claude Shannon, in the companion article to Ulam’s in the same 1958 memorial issue: “It is unfortunate that he was unable to complete the work he had in progress, some of which is in the form of rough notes or unedited lectures, and for some of which no record exists apart from his colleagues’ memories of casual conversations.” That sentence is worth reading twice. The man who founded information theory, writing the official notice of von Neumann’s automata work, states that part of it survived only in what the people around him remembered of conversation. Whatever continued after February 1957 continued through people, not documents. The report’s later sections are about which people.


## What the laboratory did, and who was there to do it

The programme needed three capacities at once: the mathematics, command of actual computing machinery and the people who ran it, and the institutional standing to direct research inside a classification regime. The laboratory’s own response is on the record, and Ulam wrote it down.

In 1957 Los Alamos created two new positions and gave one of them to him. Ulam’s account, in Adventures of a Mathematician, opens the chapter he titled Government Science and dated 1957 to 1967: “Returning to Los Alamos in 1957 after a year’s leave of absence at MIT, I was asked by Bradbury to accept one of the two newly created positions of research advisor to the director of the laboratory. The other advisor was to be John Manley … Administratively, research advisors were to be on the same level as division leaders, and their duties were to oversee the research activities throughout the laboratory, in the various divisions: theoretical, physics, chemistry and metallurgy, weapons, health, ‘Rover’ (nuclear rocket), and others. Together we tried to influence the various programs of the lab … I held this position until 1967.” In laboratory shorthand he was RASU.

The year von Neumann died, the Director of Los Alamos created two posts carrying research oversight across every division of the laboratory, at the rank of division leader, and appointed to one of them the man who had proposed the cellular framework in which von Neumann built his automaton. Ulam held it for a decade. He also records what the death changed for him personally: “A few years before Johnny’s death and increasingly so after, as a result of my work on the hydrogen bomb, I became drawn into a maze of involvements. These had to do precisely with government science and with work as a member of various Space and Air Force committees.” He is explicit that this was new for him: “Until then I had always resisted being drawn into any kind of organizational position; for years I could claim that my only administrative job had been on the Wine Tasting Committee of the Society of Fellows back in my early Harvard days.”

Metropolis held the other half. He had been at Los Alamos since April 1943, one of the original scientific staff, research associate and group leader through 1946 and staff member and group leader in the Theoretical Division from 1948 to 1957, on Carson Mark’s invitation to set up a computing facility. He was there for the ENIAC thermonuclear run, he named the Monte Carlo method, he led the group that built MANIAC I and MANIAC II, and the 1953 equation of state paper that carries his name is the origin of the algorithm that still does. In his own words to Richard Rhodes in 1993: “I was in charge of the research on computers and designed a computer.”

Teller had the standing and not the subject. In February 1957 he was associate director at Livermore, a position held since 1953, and he became its director in 1958. His domain of command was weapons physics and public advocacy for it. His one attested contribution to the computational line is co-authorship of the 1953 equation of state paper, computed on the Los Alamos MANIAC. Ulam’s own note on their relation is that “in some circles I became regarded as Teller’s opponent, and I suspect I was consulted as a sort of counterweight.”

And the machines moved with Metropolis. Eight months after von Neumann died, and the year MANIAC II was finished, he left for the University of Chicago as full professor of physics and founding director of its Institute for Computer Research. He gives the date himself: “I went twice to Chicago, once in ’46 and then another time in late ’57. I arrived in Chicago when Sputnik was —— October 4, 1957 is the date of Sputnik. I arrived in Chicago on that date and I went there and stayed there for about eight years.” There he designed MANIAC III, whose novel feature was unnormalised significance arithmetic — the subject of the first two papers he would later write with Rota. He returned to Los Alamos in 1965.

By 1966 the pieces are in the same place. Ulam is in the Director’s office with oversight of research across the laboratory, in a post he holds until 1967. Metropolis is back at Los Alamos with the machines, having built three of them. Ulam had met Rota in the early sixties. And Rota’s own curriculum vitae dates his consultancy from 1966, with the Director’s Office fellowship following in 1971. The technical output of that arrangement is itself on the record: sixteen joint publications by Rota and Metropolis between 1973 and 1995, of which the six on computation run consecutively from 1973 to 1978 and stop.

One further passage from Ulam bears on what a record like this can and cannot show. Writing about von Neumann’s last months, when the ICBM committee met in his house and then at the hotel near Walter Reed, Ulam says: “During his illness Johnny did not talk to me about his important work on the ICBM Committee; only later I learned that he was Chairman and that it was called the von Neumann Committee … To the end he maintained this complete discretion. Even though I was perhaps one of his closest friends he never broached to me classified or military subjects in which I was not involved. Our usual conversations were either about mathematics or about his new interest in a theory of automata.” The closest friend of the man at the centre did not know what he chaired until afterward. Compartmentation inside this circle is not an assumption this report makes; it is a condition its members describe.

Ulam also states, in the same chapter, why the machines existed at all: “It is evident that Johnny’s ideas on a future theory of automata and organisms had roots that went back in time, but his more concrete ideas developed after his involvement with electronic machines. I think that one of his motives for pressing for the development of electronic computers was his fascination with the working of the nervous system and the organization of the brain itself. After his death some of his collaborators collected his writings on the outlines of the theory of automata. Published posthumously, his book on the brain had merely the barest sketches of what he planned to think about. He died so prematurely, seeing the promised land but hardly entering it.”

And there is the management style of the division itself, from the same pages. Ulam on J. Carson Mark, leader of the Theoretical Division from the middle forties and the man who brought Metropolis in to build the computing facility: his direction “was an example of intelligent management of a scientific group without exercising undue pressures for programmatic work. He was able to encourage free scientific pursuits in areas which were only indirectly related to the tasks of the laboratory, and he supported theoretical physics and applied mathematics in the best sense.” Both 1958 memorial articles describe von Neumann’s work as unfinished and stop there, and no contemporaneous source located names a successor to it. What the record describes is an institution that created the oversight posts in the year of the death, kept the machines and the people, and ran a division in which work indirectly related to its stated tasks was deliberately encouraged.


### Central finding: the Super’s computational program continued as a government research effort in discrete computation and artificial intelligence, with Gian-Carlo Rota in an authorized organizing and interpretive role

From 1966 until his death in 1999, Gian-Carlo Rota held a role with Los Alamos National Laboratory and the institutional remnants of the Manhattan Project. He states the start date himself: his curriculum vitae lists, under Consultant, “Los Alamos Scientific Laboratory, 1966–,” and under Honors, “Director’s Office Fellow, Los Alamos National Laboratory, 1971–.” Los Alamos Science described him in 1985 as a seventeen year consultant and in 1987 as a consultant of over twenty years. Rota was publicly known as a Professor of Applied Mathematics and Philosophy at MIT working in abstract areas of mathematics such as combinatorics. Using only the public record, this report reconstructs the essentially unknown role he played in artificial intelligence, computational physics, and still classified work in those areas.

Thesis: A reasonable jury applying a more probable than not standard to the complete public fact pattern would conclude that the Manhattan Project’s work on the thermonuclear Super generated a continuing government program of research into computation, quantum physics, nonlinear dynamics, complexity, and artificial intelligence, and that Gian-Carlo Rota occupied an authorized organizing and interpretive role near its center. 

Counter Argument: The alternative reading of the facts would require Rota to have been coincidentally able to converse with individuals on subjects falling under an area regulated by the Atomic Energy Act of 1954 and “Restricted Data” which classifies “born secrets”, and to have done so regarding the exact subject matter in the 1979 test case of US vs the Progressive related to the “Super” hydrogen-bomb work. The counter argument would claim it coincidental that: Rota received an NSA Medal for secret work; in 1979 Rota entirely stopped publishing on related subject matter in academic journals; and that Rota was advising on hires, giving introduction speeches at LANL conferences, and editing the computational theory related books of central Super project players like Metropolis and Ulam. The alternative reading would also require that Rota’s 33 years of senior level LANL work be regarded as “incidental” just because the record of it is classified.

Analysis: The counter argument would represent an extraordinarily unlikely sequence of coincidental events in the context of the most regulated area of science where under the Atomic Energy Act of 1954 the government has successfully argued that even the 1st Amendment protections to speech do not apply.

The lineage begins with ENIAC’s first large scale program, a Los Alamos thermonuclear feasibility calculation, and with Monte Carlo methods for stochastic neutron transport. It then divides into two alternative routes taken by men who knew each other’s ideas and chose differently. Ulam, von Neumann and Metropolis took the discrete route: probabilistic and combinatorial machines, cellular automata, Monte Carlo, and a theory of automata that von Neumann intended as a replacement for the continuous formalism, not an approximation to it. Feynman took the continuous route: he granted the quantum formalism as correct and asked what physical hardware could obey it. Feynman named the split himself in the discussion following his 1981 keynote — “Discretizing is the right word. Quantizing is a different kind of mathematics.” The routes are alternatives, not halves of one design. Rota connected the people, technical subjects, conferences, publications, classified forums, and government institutions in which the discrete route was developed and interpreted, while the continuous route ran through open academic physics.


### The circle and what they said they were building

The people are a specific and countable group, and they are the same group across four decades: John von Neumann, Stanislaw Ulam, Enrico Fermi, Nicholas Metropolis, Edward Teller, Robert Richtmyer, John Pasta and Mary Tsingou at Los Alamos, joined from the late nineteen sixties by Gian-Carlo Rota. Richard Feynman belongs to the same wartime formation and is the control case: he took the other route and went to Caltech. What distinguishes the group is not that they worked at one laboratory. It is that they said, in print and in their own words, that they were building a new science, and they described the same one.

Ulam, in the 1958 Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society memorial, on what von Neumann had been attempting when he died: “it was toward the end of his life that he felt sure enough of himself to engage freely and yet painstakingly in the creation of a possible new mathematical discipline. This was to be a combinatorial theory of automata and organisms.” Rota, in Los Alamos Science in 1985, speaking as a participant and not a surveyor: “a new unit in science is being formed that remains to be named. It will include the best of theoretical computer science, neurophysiology, molecular biology, psychology, and the mathematical theory of information … We need to name it glamorously, to make it into a respectable profession, and to bring people together to work on it. This is beginning to happen. It is the central drama of today’s science.”

Twenty seven years apart, at the same laboratory, two men describe the same unnamed discipline out of the same ingredients. That is the spine of this report, and it is the reason the central claim is not a conjecture imposed on the record from outside. The participants stated the program themselves. What remains to be established is the institutional question: who paid for it, who convened it, and why the field they both describe as the central drama of contemporary science left almost no publication trail.

## Executive assessment

The thesis is a cumulative inference from conduct, access, subject matter, institutional continuity, and legally structured secrecy. Each item can be imagined separately, but a jury evaluates the whole pattern. The combination is extraordinarily unlikely to have arisen through unorganized academic contact: Rota held recurring Los Alamos appointments and Director’s Office access, collected private scientific briefings, organized the relevant conferences, edited the relevant intellectual record, collaborated technically with Metropolis, represented Los Alamos in a classified Intelligence Community AI forum, wrote classified national security reports, served in international security work, and received an NSA distinction for classified service.


### What is stated, and what is argued

Most of what follows is not a thesis. It is testimony, given in print, by the men who did the work, over their own names.

Ulam, writing von Neumann’s memorial in 1958, said that von Neumann had been working toward a new branch of science that had grown out of the computing methods and the approaches the two of them had developed together at Los Alamos, and that he himself was part of it. Ulam then wrote a book about that work, Science, Computers, and People. Rota edited it. Nicholas Metropolis was working on the same material and brought out the volume on it, A History of Computing in the Twentieth Century, which he and Rota edited together with Jack Howlett from a Los Alamos conference of June 1976. Rota, for his part, said in print in 1985 that the discipline he was working in had not yet been named, and remarked that nomen est omen.

Rota’s sustained and authorized classified service is documented in the same way, and is likewise not argued here. A declassified Intelligence Community symposium program and its summary report place him in a classified artificial intelligence forum as a Los Alamos Director’s Office Fellow. The National Academy memoir records the senior fellowship and states that the basis of his National Security Agency Medal for Distinguished Service is classified. The American Mathematical Society memorial and Michael Waterman’s account record classified national security reports and advisory service.

Against that testimony stands one further fact of the same kind, equally undisputed. There is not a single peer reviewed paper, by any of them, setting out what the new science is. It has no name, no axioms in the open literature, and no citation trail. Three men said they were building a new science, across four decades and several books, and the reviewed scientific record contains no such science.

Two claims in this report are argued rather than stated. The first is that von Neumann directed the trajectory of classified United States research toward that new science, and that its content was held within the group that had done the Super work. The second is that Rota’s Entropy Theorem is a central theorem in it. Everything else set out below is evidence — appointments, clearances, conference programs, editorial roles, publication records, collaborations, award citations — put before the reader on the ordinary understanding that the reader weighs it.

### Why the network claim is persuasive

The network begins with a concrete computational need on an extraordinarily difficult quantum computing problem – calculating the quantum mechanics of neutron chain reactions. Los Alamos weapons physics required the reduction of coupled physical processes to numerical procedures that could be executed first by human computers and punched card machines and then by ENIAC and the MANIAC family. John von Neumann, Stanislaw Ulam, Nicholas Metropolis, Robert Richtmyer, Edward Teller, Enrico Fermi, and later Richard Feynman appear not as a retrospective list of famous names but as participants in overlapping problems, institutions, machines, and conferences. Monte Carlo grew from neutron transport and related weapons calculations. The Fermi Pasta Ulam Tsingou experiment used MANIAC to expose unexpected structure in a nonlinear dynamical system. Los Alamos later became a major home of complex systems research. These are documented connections. The complexity at issue is the one the circle actually engaged: physical intractability, the resources a finite machine needs to reproduce a physical process, and the emergent behaviour of nonlinear systems. Rota’s 1977 paper on carry propagation proposed the bridge from that work to formal computational complexity in so many words.


### Why Rota matters

Rota’s role is unusually visible at the level of intellectual organization. The National Academy memoir records that he became a Los Alamos senior fellow in 1971 and spent several weeks there every year. Los Alamos Science described him in 1985 as a seventeen year consultant. He coedited the proceedings of the 1976 Los Alamos history of computing conference, participated in the laboratory’s information and AI discussions, memorialized Ulam at Los Alamos, and appeared in a United States Intelligence Community AI symposium as a Los Alamos Director’s Office Fellow. The National Security Agency awarded him its Medal for Distinguished Service in 1992; the National Academy memoir says the basis was classified. Michael Waterman’s memorial account adds service on a Los Alamos nonproliferation and international security advisory board and classified national security reports.


### The cumulative inference

The relevant question is not whether any single conversation could have been unclassified. It is whether the entire configuration could reasonably arise without an authorized role. In a laboratory governed by clearances, need to know rules, Restricted Data controls, and compartmented national security work, friendship with Ulam does not explain Director’s Office status, systematic private briefings, high level laboratory politics, classified reports, Intelligence Community AI participation, and classified NSA recognition. The unavailable charter, reports, remarks, and award basis are the very records that would state the role directly. Their known classification makes the evidentiary record incomplete in a predictable direction.


## Von Neumann’s authority, 1949 to 1957

One proposition in this report is not an inference and should be settled before anything else is argued. In the last years of his life John von Neumann was not an eminent scientist who advised the United States government. He was inside the machinery that set the government’s scientific and technical research agenda, and the documents that show it are official, public, and specific.


### The ICBM decision

On 8 September 1955 the National Security Council met for the 258th time. The attendance list, in the memorandum of discussion held at the Eisenhower Library, records “Dr. John von Neumann for the Chairman, Atomic Energy Commission.” The Council resolved that “the U. S. ICBM program is therefore a program of the highest priority above all others, except as directed by the President,” and that “the Secretary of Defense will prosecute the program with all practicable speed, and all other Executive departments and agencies will assist the Department of Defense as required.” Before it did so, the memorandum records this exchange: “The Vice President then turned to Dr. von Neumann and asked him whether, in his judgment, the proposed NSC action on the ICBM program ‘went far enough’.” Von Neumann answered that the wording should read “with all possible speed” rather than “all practicable speed,” because “the precise choice of words became much more important the further down the line you went.” He proposed in addition a report to the Council on avenues of approach not being explored. General Twining, Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, “said that he was greatly impressed with Dr. von Neumann’s idea,” and the Council “accepted the amendment to subparagraph d which inserted the phrase ‘including avenues not explored’.”

A private citizen is not asked by the Vice President of the United States whether a draft presidential directive goes far enough, and does not have his amendment written into the text of the highest priority research program in the country.


### The committee that produced it

The directive did not arrive at the Council by accident. The official history of the United States Air Force — Jacob Neufeld, The Development of Ballistic Missiles in the United States Air Force, 1945–1960, Office of Air Force History — records that a nuclear weapons panel of the Air Force Scientific Advisory Board was established in March 1953 “with Dr. John von Neumann … as Chairman,” and that in October 1953 Trevor Gardner “recruited eleven leading scientists and engineers to participate in the Strategic Missiles Evaluation Group, also known as the Teapot Committee. Dr. von Neumann served as Chairman.” The committee reported on 10 February 1954 and called for a “radical reorganization of the … project considerably transcending the Convair framework.” On 14 May 1954 the Air Force Vice Chief of Staff “assigned the Air Force’s highest research and development priority to the ATLAS project.” On 28 July 1955, in the words of the same official history, “Gardner, von Neumann, and Schriever made the presentation” to the President and the National Security Council. On 13 September 1955 the President approved. Eisenhower wrote that day that “no other development program is now the subject of so urgent and emphatic a directive.”

The committee he chaired was then given standing authority across the whole Department of Defense. Neufeld, again: it “was transformed into an advisory body of the Department of Defense. It was provided with complete access to all technological developments and made subject to call by all of the military services and OSD for technical program review and advice. This action provided that ‘the Air Force ballistic missile programs [would] be subject to no other outside scientific consultant review.’” The official organization chart for October 1955 places the Scientific Advisory Committee at the top of the structure, captioned “Prof von Neumann, Chairman.”


### The Commission

Nominated to the Atomic Energy Commission in October 1954 and confirmed after a delay caused by Joint Committee unease over his association with Oppenheimer, von Neumann took office on 15 March 1955. The Commission’s own Eighteenth Semiannual Report to Congress records it — “Dr. John von Neumann took office as Commissioner on March 15, following his confirmation by the Senate” — and carries his signature on the letter of submittal to the President of the Senate and the Speaker of the House. Under the Atomic Energy Act of 1954 the Commission was five members with one vote each, charged with “a program of conducting, assisting, and fostering research and development” and with “government control of the possession, use, and production of atomic energy,” operating the national laboratories and, under section 91, the production of atomic weapons. He had already served on the Commission’s General Advisory Committee, the statutory body constituted “to advise the Commission on scientific and technical matters relating to materials, production, and research and development,” from 27 February 1952 to 1 August 1954, by presidential appointment.


### Seventeen years of standing positions

The Commission seat was the culmination of a continuous record, printed by Ulam in the Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society and independently corroborated by the American Institute of Physics and by the Library of Congress finding aid to his papers: Scientific Advisory Committee, Ballistic Research Laboratories, Aberdeen, 1940–1957; Navy Bureau of Ordnance, 1941–1955; consultant to Los Alamos, 1943–1955; Naval Ordnance Laboratory, 1947–1955; the Research and Development Board, 1949–1953; Oak Ridge, 1949–1954; Weapons Systems Evaluation Group and the Armed Forces Special Weapons Project, 1950–1955; the Scientific Advisory Board of the United States Air Force, 1951–1957; the General Advisory Committee, 1952–1954; the Technical Advisory Panel on Atomic Energy, 1953–1957; and Chairman of the Advisory Committee on Guided Missiles, 1954–1957.

The Research and Development Board is the one to read the statute on. It was created by the National Security Act of 1947, and section 214 gave it the duty “to prepare a complete and integrated program of research and development for military purposes,” “to advise with regard to trends in scientific research relating to national security and the measures necessary to assure continued and increasing progress,” “to recommend measures of coordination of research and development among the military departments,” and “to formulate policy for the National Military Establishment in connection with research and development matters involving agencies outside the National Military Establishment.” Its first chairman was Vannevar Bush, of whom Truman wrote that “the coordination of our scientific effort is one of the most important objectives of the National Security Act.” Von Neumann sat on that Board from 1949 to 1953.


### What the government said it was recognizing

On 15 February 1956 Eisenhower presented him the Medal of Freedom. The citation, in the Public Papers of the Presidents, is headed “for exceptionally meritorious service in furtherance of the security of the United States” and reads: “Dr. von Neumann, in a series of scientific study projects of major national significance, has materially increased the scientific progress of this country in the armaments field. Through his work on various highly classified missions performed outside the continental limits of the United States in conjunction with critically important international programs, Dr. von Neumann has resolved some of the most difficult technical problems of national defense.” It is the only document in Eisenhower’s entire public papers for 1954 to 1957 that names him.

In the same year he became the first recipient of the Enrico Fermi Award, a presidential award. The Department of Energy still publishes the citation: “For his scientific contribution to the theory of fast computing machines and for his original contributions to their design and construction. More than anyone else he foresaw the important and necessary role they would play in the control and use of atomic energy and to the general advancement of the arts and sciences for the benefit of mankind.” The government’s highest scientific honor, in its first year, went to von Neumann, and the reason the government gave was computing machines and the fact that he saw before anyone else what they were for.


### The last year

Life magazine, in an obituary published three weeks after his death, described him as “the principal adviser to the U.S. Air Force on nuclear weapons” and “the most influential scientific force behind the U.S. decision to embark on accelerated production of intercontinental ballistic missiles,” and reported the conditions of his illness: a telephone by his bed connected directly to his Commission office; trips downtown by limousine to attend Commission meetings in a wheelchair; an Air Force officer, Lieutenant Colonel Vincent Ford, assigned full time; eight airmen cleared for top secret material on twenty four hour duty. “Cabinet members and military officials continually came for his advice, and on one occasion Secretary of Defence Charles Wilson, Air Force Secretary Donald Quarles and most of the top Air Force brass gathered in Von Neumann’s suite to consult his judgement while there was still time.” The grander version of that scene, in which the Joint Chiefs and all three service secretaries attend, comes from Lewis Strauss rather than from Life. The Air Force’s own official history supplies the corroboration that matters: it records a meeting of his Scientific Advisory Committee “held at Walter Reed Army Hospital, as von Neumann was about to undergo cancer surgery” — the session whose recommendation became the Thor missile.

That is the man whose late program was a combinatorial theory of automata and organisms, who told Ulam the differential equations were no longer sacrosanct, and who spent his last two years writing an unfinished book about a machine architecture that did not exist. The question this report takes up is what became of that program after February 1957.


## What he believed, and how far it was from the orthodoxy

The man described in the preceding section held views on the foundations of mathematics and of physics that were directly contrary to what was being taught and published in both fields. He stated them in print, under his own name, while holding the positions described above.


### Mathematics needed to be refounded

“The Mathematician,” published in 1947 in R. B. Heywood’s The Works of the Mind, is the essay in which he set out the problem, and the position it takes is that classical mathematics has no secure foundation and is used anyway. In his own words: “The main hope of a justification of classical mathematics — in the sense of Hilbert or of Brouwer and Weyl — being gone, most mathematicians decided to use that system anyway. After all, classical mathematics was producing results which were both elegant and useful, and, even though one could never again be absolutely certain of its reliability, it stood on at least as sound a foundation as, for example, the existence of the electron. Hence, if one was willing to accept the sciences, one might as well accept the classical system of mathematics.” On Hilbert’s programme: “My personal opinion, which is shared by many others, is, that Gödel has shown that Hilbert’s program is essentially hopeless.”

The alternative was available and he states its price exactly: “A system of mathematics which was free of these undesirable traits, ‘intuitionism,’ was developed by Brouwer. In this system the difficulties and contradiction of set theory did not arise. However, a good fifty per cent of modern mathematics, in its most vital — and up to then unquestioned — parts, especially in analysis, were also affected by this ‘purge’: they either became invalid or had to be justified by very complicated subsidiary considerations. And in this latter process one usually lost appreciably in generality of validity and elegance of deduction.” Half of the subject, gone or salvaged at a cost, and Brouwer and Weyl held that mathematical rigour should be revised accordingly. This is the state of the foundations as von Neumann described it to a general readership in 1947: the classical system unjustified, the rigorous replacement ruinous, and the profession proceeding on the classical one because it worked.

He says why he has told it at that length: “I think that it constitutes the best caution against taking the immovable rigour of mathematics too much for granted. This happened in our own lifetime, and I know myself how humiliatingly easily my own views regarding the absolute mathematical truth changed during this episode, and how they changed three times in succession!”

His position on intuitionism was not a bystander’s, and he had put it to the one person whose authority on the matter is now treated as settled. On 12 January 1931, writing to Kurt Gödel from Berlin — months after the incompleteness result and while von Neumann was among the first to grasp it — he stated a flat disagreement: “I absolutely disagree with your view on the formalizability of intuitionism. Certainly, for every formal system there is, as you proved, another formal one that is (already in arithmetic and the lower functional calculus) stronger. But intuitionism is not affected by that at all.” He presses the point: “Clearly I cannot prove that every intuitionistically correct construction of arithmetic is formalizable in A or M or even in Z — for intuitionism is undefined and undefinable. But is it not a fact, that not a single construction of the kind mentioned is known that cannot be formalized in A, and that no living logician is in the position of naming such [a construction]? Or am I wrong, and you know an effective intuitionistic arithmetic construction whose formalization in A creates difficulties?” In the same letter he reports a decision method of his own for a class of provability statements. The exchange is not a dispute about a detail. Gödel’s theorems are now taught as the boundary of what formal systems can do; von Neumann, in 1931, told their author to his face that the boundary did not fall where Gödel placed it with respect to intuitionism.

The positive doctrine of the essay is that mathematics is empirical in origin and decays when it forgets that. “Mathematical ideas originate in empirics, although the genealogy is sometimes long and obscure.” And the warning: “As a mathematical discipline travels far from its empirical source, or still more, if it is a second and third generation only indirectly inspired by ideas coming from ‘reality’ it is beset with very grave dangers. It becomes more and more purely aestheticizing, more and more purely l’art pour l’art … In other words, at a great distance from its empirical source, or after much ‘abstract’ inbreeding, a mathematical subject is in danger of degeneration. At the inception the style is usually classical; when it shows signs of becoming baroque, then the danger signal is up.” The remedy: “the rejuvenating return to the source: the re-injection of more or less directly empirical ideas.”

Ten years later, in the book he did not finish, he took the argument to its end. Mathematics is not a discovered necessity but a historical accident: “Just as languages like Greek or Sanskrit are historical facts and not absolute logical necessities, it is only reasonable to assume that logics and mathematics are similarly historical, accidental forms of expression.” And the nervous system does not use it: “whatever language the central nervous system is using, it is characterized by less logical and arithmetical depth than what we are normally used to,” and that language “may well correspond to a short code … rather than to a complete code: when we talk mathematics, we may be discussing a secondary language, built on the primary language truly used by the central nervous system.” Ulam, reviewing the same passages, notes that this second language “must be quite different from languages in the usual sense. It may, for example, operate with analogies.”


### Physics: the formalism he wrote was not one he believed

Von Neumann gave quantum mechanics its rigorous mathematical form in 1932 and then spent the following decades trying to replace it. To Garrett Birkhoff, 13 November 1935: “I would like to make a confession which may seem immoral: I do not believe absolutely in Hilbert space any more. After all, Hilbert space (as far as quantum mechanical things are concerned) was obtained by generalizing Euclidean space, based on the principle of ‘conserving the validity of all formal rules’ … Now we begin to believe that it is not the vectors that matter, but the lattice of all linear (closed) subspaces. Because: 1) The vectors ought to represent the physical states, but they do so redundantly, up to a complex factor, only 2) and besides, the states are merely a derived notion, the primitive (phenomenologically given) notion being the qualities which correspond to the linear closed subspaces.” The correct infinite dimensional generalization, he held, was not Hilbert space but the type II-one factor. To Dirac, 27 January 1934, he had already proposed quantizing space itself: “It should be perhaps desirable, to have operators X, Y, Z which have discrete (point) spectra, in order to avoid the difficulties connected with the point electron.” Ulam, who had the conversations and wrote the memorial, records his own estimate of the same work in Adventures of a Mathematician: “As for myself, I was never greatly impressed by his work on Hilbert space or on continuous geometry. This is a question of taste, and when I was more of a purist myself I made good-natured fun of certain of his involvements in applications.”

On the central claim of the orthodox interpretation he was explicit, in writing, in 1936. To Schrödinger, 11 April 1936: “I cannot accept your § 4. completely. I think that the difficulties you hint at are ‘pseudo-problems’. The ‘action at distance’ in the case under consideration says only that even if there is no dynamical interaction between two systems (e.g. because they are far removed from each other), the systems can display statistical correlations. This is not at all specific for quantum mechanics, it happens classically as well.” He then gives the example: two boxes, a white ball placed in each or a black ball placed in each a million years ago, nobody knowing which; one box buried on Earth, the other on Sirius. “Now one digs S1 on Earth out, opens it and sees: the ball is white. This action on Earth changes instantaneously the S2 statistic on Sirius.” And the joke he attaches to it, about the colonel cuckolded in Madagascar at the moment something happened to his wife in Paris — what changes at a distance is a semantic fact, not a physical one. Nothing propagates. The correlation is between states already fixed, and the probabilities are measures of ignorance about them. That is the reading of the wave function the standard interpretation exists to deny.

He also rejected the frequency interpretation of the probabilities his own formalism produced. From the unfinished manuscript on quantum logics in his papers at the Library of Congress: “This view, the so-called ‘frequency theory of probability’ has been very brilliantly upheld and expounded by R. von Mises. This view, however, is not acceptable to us, at least not in the present ‘logical’ context.” And in 1955, on the great interpretive controversy of the age, he declined to treat it as a question about truth at all: “while there appears to be a serious philosophical controversy between the interpretations of Schrödinger and Heisenberg, it is quite likely that the controversy will be settled quite an unphilosophical way. The decision is likely to be opportunistic in the end. The theory that lends itself better to formalistic extension towards valid new theories will overcome the other … It must be emphasized that this is not a question of accepting the correct theory and rejecting the false one. It is a matter of accepting that theory which shows greater formal adaptability for a correct extension. This is a formalistic, esthetic criterion, with a highly opportunistic flavor.” That is not a defence of the standard interpretation. It is a statement that the question of which interpretation is true had ceased to interest him, because he expected the framework itself to be superseded.

Ulam, who had the conversations, records where this left him relative to Einstein — and the comparison is the point, because in the nineteen fifties the Copenhagen interpretation of Bohr and Heisenberg was not one reading among several but the settled position of the discipline, and Einstein’s refusal of it was treated as the eccentricity of a great man past his best work. From the 1958 memorial: “beginning about 1938, von Neumann felt that the new facts and problems of nuclear physics gave rise to problems of an entirely different type and made it less important to insist on a mathematically flawless formulation of a quantum theory of atomic phenomena alone. After the end of the war, he would express sentiments, somewhat similar to remarks reportedly made by Einstein, that the bewildering wealth of nuclear and elementary particle physics make premature any attempt to formulate a general quantum theory of fields, at least for the time being.” The man who had written the mathematical foundations of quantum mechanics is placed by his closest collaborator, in the official memorial, on Einstein’s side of the question. Ulam adds, of what von Neumann selected as his own most important contributions: “perhaps his main desire and one of his strongest motivations was to help re-establish the role of mathematics on a conceptual level in theoretical physics.”


### The circle’s view of Copenhagen

The attitude was not von Neumann’s alone. Ulam, in Adventures of a Mathematician, sets out his own position on complementarity after a paragraph of warm praise for Bohr personally — “Somehow he seemed the embodiment of wisdom. (Wisdom, perhaps not genius in the sense of Newton or Einstein.)” — and then: “He had his own kind of genius that made him a great physicist but, to my mind, some of his students were almost benighted by his complementarity philosophy of ‘one can say this, but on the other hand one can …’ or ‘one cannot say sharply what this means.’ People without his great sense and intuitive wisdom were led astray and lost the precision and sharpness of their intellectual or scientific approach, in my opinion … It seems to me that as a philosophical guideline, complementarity is essentially negative. It can only console. Whether it can be positively useful other than in philosophical consolations is a question which troubles me.”

In the same passage Ulam reports Fermi, and notes that the remark was out of character: “Departing from his usual caution about expressing opinions about other people, Fermi remarked once that when Bohr talked he sometimes gave the impression of a Catholic priest celebrating mass.” Ulam calls this “an iconoclastic statement, since so many physicists are still under the spell of Bohr.” Ulam also records Fermi’s own dissatisfaction with the formalism, walking in Frijoles Canyon: told that the Schrödinger equation agreed with experiment to six decimal places, Fermi said, “It has no business being that good, you know, Stan.” Fermi then set out to give a rational derivation of quantum theory in his Chicago course, and reported the following summer: “No, I didn’t succeed to my satisfaction in giving a really rational introduction to quantum theory.”


### The divergence

Set the two records side by side. Through the nineteen fifties and after, academic physics proceeded on the standard interpretation and on continuous field theories whose equations could not be solved; Feynman’s 1981 proposal, which granted the quantum formalism as correct and asked what hardware could obey it, is the mature expression of that route. Meanwhile the man who chaired the committee that made the ICBM the highest priority program in the United States, who sat on the Atomic Energy Commission, and who received the first Enrico Fermi Award for his work on computing machines, held that mathematics needed refounding on intuitionist lines at a cost of half of analysis, that logic and arithmetic were historical accidents not used by the brain, that he no longer believed in the Hilbert space he had built for quantum mechanics, and that the interpretive controversy would be settled opportunistically rather than on the truth. The people from the Super who remained in government service held the same view of the orthodoxy: Ulam on complementarity as something that “can only console,” Fermi on the Schrödinger equation having “no business being that good.”

That divergence is the setting for everything that follows in this report. Two intellectual programs, one public and one inside the laboratory, and the man with the authority to direct federal research was on the second.

The analysis uses “quantum computing” in two historically related senses that must remain distinct. ENIAC, MANIAC, and early Monte Carlo were classical machines and methods used to calculate processes governed by quantum and nuclear physics. Feynman’s later proposal was different in kind: a universal simulator whose own operation obeyed quantum mechanics. The report argues that these are alternative answers to the same deep problem of making nature computationally tractable, not that ENIAC was a quantum computer in the modern hardware sense, and not that the two answers were designed to fit together. Complexity likewise includes physical intractability, formal computational resources, and the emergent behavior of nonlinear systems; Rota’s 1977 work on carry propagation expressly proposed a connection to computational complexity.

## The wartime and thermonuclear computing nucleus

**Established facts**

Los Alamos brought theoretical physics, applied mathematics, engineering, and large scale calculation into a single weapons laboratory. The need to model neutron transport, shock propagation, radiation, and thermonuclear burn encouraged methods that treated physical theory as an executable numerical procedure. That institutional arrangement created repeated contact among von Neumann, Ulam, Metropolis, Richtmyer, Teller, Fermi, and many less celebrated programmers and human computers.


### ENIAC and the thermonuclear calculation

The ENIAC calculation is the clearest bridge between the Super and the later computational program. A recent Los Alamos history identifies the Los Alamos thermonuclear feasibility study as the first large scale program ever run on ENIAC. The machine’s inaugural major scientific task was therefore not a generic benchmark but an attempt to make an otherwise unmanageable quantum physical and thermonuclear system calculable. Von Neumann arranged the run; Teller’s group supplied the Super problem; Frankel and other Los Alamos personnel programmed it; and Metropolis, Ulam, Richtmyer, and their colleagues belonged to the same computational formation.

The calculation was classical and severely simplified. It did not produce the later Teller Ulam configuration, and it could not represent the full quantum state of the physical system. That limitation is the important continuity. From the beginning, the research problem was not only how to build a faster calculator but how to choose representations, probabilities, approximations, and physical machinery capable of simulating nature beyond direct analytic solution.


### Monte Carlo

Monte Carlo methods emerged as the classical stochastic answer to the neutron transport problem. Ulam, von Neumann, Richtmyer, and Metropolis represented the quantum physical history of individual neutrons by random sampling and estimated the aggregate behavior of diffusion and multiplication. The 1947 Los Alamos report on statistical methods in neutron diffusion and the 1949 Metropolis and Ulam paper document the early formulation. Los Alamos histories place these advances inside a continuous progression from Manhattan Project neutronics to postwar thermonuclear weapons computation.

The method is older than the name, and the hardware progression is the point. Enrico Fermi was solving neutron problems by statistical sampling by hand in Rome in the early nineteen thirties, years before the technique acquired a code name; Metropolis credited him with it. At Los Alamos that practice was embodied in a physical device, the hand cranked FERMIAC of 1947, an analog trolley that traced neutron histories across a scale drawing of a reactor assembly. The sequence FERMIAC, ENIAC, MANIAC is therefore not a list of successively faster calculators. It is one method — follow individual random histories and aggregate them — migrating from hand computation to an analog mechanism to a general purpose electronic machine, while the mathematics stayed the same. What 1946 marks is not the invention of sampling but the moment the method became a computer algorithm on an electronic machine and acquired its name. The significance for this report is that the group’s commitment was to the algorithm rather than to any particular hardware, and that commitment is what later makes a stochastic machine thinkable.

The method’s genealogy is both technical and institutional. Neutron transport and weapons related calculations supplied difficult problems; electronic machines made repeated sampling practical; mathematicians and physicists supplied probabilistic models; and Los Alamos supplied a setting where the method could circulate across disciplines. Later applications spread far beyond weapons work. The method was devised at Los Alamos, on weapons calculations, by the people named above.


### Monte Carlo becomes the discrete lattice, at Brookhaven

The method did not stay at Los Alamos. On 26 to 28 April 1948, at an Atomic Energy Commission
information meeting at Brookhaven National Laboratory, Ulam presented “Statistical Methods for
Problems Involving Equations of the Diffusion Type (Monte Carlo)” — the method carried to a second
national laboratory in the year after that laboratory opened.

Thirty-one years later the same laboratory used it to make quantum field theory computable. In 1979
Michael Creutz, Laurence Jacobs and Claudio Rebbi, all of the Physics Department at Brookhaven,
published “Experiments with a Gauge-Invariant Ising System” and then “Monte Carlo Study of Abelian
Lattice Gauge Theories,” which carries the laboratory report number BNL-26307; in April 1980 Creutz
published “Monte Carlo Study of Quantized SU(2) Gauge Theory.” The construction is the one this
report has been following: take a continuous quantum field theory, replace spacetime with a discrete
lattice, and generate the field configurations by random sampling. Brookhaven treats that work as
foundational in its own account. Dedicating a supercomputer in December 2005, the laboratory
simultaneously marked the “25th anniversary of a scientific paper describing the first use of Monte
Carlo methods and lattice gauge calculations in the study of quantum chromodynamics (QCD).”

The machines that followed were built for that calculation and for nothing else. QCDSP, built with
Columbia University and installed at Brookhaven in 1998, was a massively parallel computer intended
primarily for lattice gauge simulations, and took the Gordon Bell Prize for price and performance
that year. Its successor QCDOC — QCD On a Chip — was designed around the same calculation, its
architecture described by its builders as “optimized for lattice QCD calculations,” and began
operation at Brookhaven in 2005.

Rota was a consultant to Brookhaven National Laboratory from 1969 to 1973, by his own curriculum
vitae, in the same years he consulted for the RAND Corporation and for Los Alamos. The
sampling method Ulam proposed and von Neumann took up was presented by Ulam at Brookhaven in 1948,
and Brookhaven is where that method was turned on the continuum of quantum field theory and where
the machines for doing so were then built.

**Circumstantial implication**

The overlap is too dense to treat the fields as independent developments that merely happened at the same laboratory. The first large scale ENIAC program, Monte Carlo neutron transport, MANIAC, nonlinear dynamics, cellular automata, Boolean arithmetic, computational complexity, quantum simulation, and AI are connected by recurring personnel and a continuing Los Alamos problem: how to represent, compute, and understand physical processes whose state spaces defeat direct calculation. The lineage is visible without applying any later label to it.


## From weapons calculation to nonlinear dynamics and complexity


### The Fermi Pasta Ulam Tsingou experiment

In 1953 to 1955, Fermi, John Pasta, Ulam, and programmer Mary Tsingou used MANIAC I to study a chain of oscillators with nonlinear interactions. The expected drift toward equipartition did not occur. Energy returned in striking recurrences. The Los Alamos report LA 1940 recorded the result, which became foundational for nonlinear dynamics, solitons, and computational experiment. The episode is especially probative because the work used a weapons laboratory computer and personnel whose computational practice had developed in the laboratory’s defense mission.

A later Department of Energy historical study expressly describes the work as growing from mathematical treatments of thermonuclear weapons and identifies Los Alamos as an origin point for complex systems studies. That statement supports institutional and methodological continuity.


### Discrete computing and complexity

Digital weapons calculation required discretization: continuous physical fields were represented by meshes, time steps, finite differences, sampled histories, and finite machine states. Rota’s mature mathematics approached discrete structures as objects worthy of theory rather than approximations to continuous mathematics. His work in combinatorics, incidence algebras, Möbius inversion, matroids, and finite operator calculus supplied a language for structure in discrete systems. This is a real intellectual affinity, but no public source reviewed here shows that his combinatorial results were commissioned for thermonuclear design.

The phrase computational complexity creates a risk of anachronism. Los Alamos scientists certainly confronted calculations that were computationally difficult and later became leaders in complex systems research. Formal complexity theory, however, is a more specific discipline concerned with resources required by algorithms. The evidence supports overlap through discrete mathematics, algorithms, automata, optimization, and intractable problems.


### Physics computation and artificial intelligence

The move from weapons simulation toward artificial intelligence followed the structure of the original problem. Both required a machine to represent an immense state space, use partial information, select tractable approximations, and produce behavior or predictions that could not be reached by direct symbolic calculation. Ulam wrote about heuristic reasoning, games, biology, cellular automata, and random methods for intractable problems. Rota described AI as an emerging synthesis of theoretical computer science, neurobiology, psychology, and information theory. The same circle moved from calculating physical behavior to asking what computation, intelligence, and understanding are.


### Three routes: Ulam and Metropolis, Feynman, and Shannon

Ulam and Metropolis developed the discrete route. Monte Carlo replaced an impossible enumeration of particle histories with stochastic sampling. Cellular automata replaced continuous physical description with local update rules on discrete states. MANIAC turned those representations into computational experiments. The crucial point, and the one the secondary literature usually softens, is that Ulam described this as a break rather than a supplement. In his 1958 memorial for von Neumann he writes that the intractability of nonlinear equations “impelled him to study new possibilities of computation on electronic machines, ab initio,” and that the electronic machines “invite the invention of entirely new methods not only in numerical analysis in the classical sense, but in the very foundations of procedures of mathematical analysis itself.” Of the physical models themselves Ulam is blunter still: “the differential equations originally obtained by certain idealizations, are not, so to say, very sacrosanct any more.” And of the method: running random walks on a machine “in fact reverses the treatment which in probability theory reduces the study of random walks to the study of differential equations.” Reversal is the operative word. Monte Carlo does not supplement the differential equation; it runs the classical reduction backwards.

Ulam’s place in this is structural rather than incidental, and the report’s earlier drafts understated it. The cellular framework in which von Neumann built his self reproducing automaton was Ulam’s suggestion. Arthur Burks, editing von Neumann’s unfinished manuscript, states it at the head of the relevant section: “Von Neumann’s second model of self-reproduction is his cellular model. It was stimulated by S. M. Ulam, who suggested during a discussion of the kinematic model that a cellular framework would be more amenable to logical and mathematical treatment than the framework of the kinematic model.” At the point in von Neumann’s own text where the attribution belongs, the footnote was left unwritten and Burks supplies it in brackets: “Von Neumann was going to refer to S. Ulam here.” Burks repeats the attribution in his own 1969 survey: “This concept was suggested to von Neumann by S. M. Ulam.” Ulam’s own contemporaneous statement is more modest and should be quoted with it — in his 1950 International Congress address he describes “a general model, considered by von Neumann and the author,” of “an infinite lattice or graph of points, each with a finite number of connections to certain of its ‘neighbors,’” each point “capable of a finite number of ‘states,’” with transitions “fixed deterministically or, more generally, … partly ‘random.’” That is the definition of a cellular automaton, published in 1950, by Ulam, describing joint work. Monte Carlo, cellular automata and the nonlinear computational experiment are all his or jointly his. He is not a mathematician adjacent to computation; he is one of the two people who defined what computation was going to be used for.

Ulam frames von Neumann’s late work the same way. Mathematicians, he writes, choose between contributing to “the edifice of existing work” and the desire “to blaze new trails and to create new syntheses,” and von Neumann, at the end of his life, took the second course — the passage quoted at the opening of this report and taken up again below. In the same obituary Ulam records his estimate of the standing theory: quantum mechanics “as conceived during these years by Schrödinger, Heisenberg, Dirac, and others still forms only an incomplete theoretical skeleton for the more baffling physical phenomena discovered since.” That is the premise of the discrete route, stated in the official Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society memorial, and it is not the premise of Feynman’s 1981 keynote.

Von Neumann’s own correspondence shows the same direction of attack a generation earlier. To Dirac, 27 January 1934, proposing to quantize space itself in order to remove the divergences: “It should be perhaps desirable, to have operators X, Y, Z which have discrete (point) spectra, in order to avoid the difficulties connected with the point electron.” The Birkhoff letter of 13 November 1935 and the Schrödinger letter of 11 April 1936, both quoted in full earlier in this report, make the same move in the other two directions: the physically primitive object is the lattice of closed subspaces rather than the vector, and nonlocality is a pseudo-problem. The man who axiomatized quantum mechanics spent the following decade trying to replace the mathematics he had axiomatized. That is the intellectual root of the discrete route, and it is incompatible with a program whose first move is to accept the formalism as final.

Feynman took the other route, and he took it knowingly. In “Simulating Physics with Computers,” delivered in May 1981 and published in 1982, he opens with universal computers and cellular automata, considers the discrete option explicitly — “we might change the idea that space is continuous to the idea that space perhaps is a simple lattice and everything is discrete” — and then declines it on experimental grounds: “the first difficulty that would come out is that the speed of light would depend slightly on the direction, and there might be other anisotropies in the physics that we could detect experimentally.” Having set that aside he states his premise: “I’m going to suppose that quantum mechanics is correct,” and later, “I don’t know, maybe physics is absolutely OK the way it is.” He then shows that a classical probabilistic machine cannot reproduce quantum correlations — “it is impossible to represent the results of quantum mechanics with a classical universal device” — and concludes that the simulator itself must exploit quantum mechanical behavior. This is why Feynman is properly treated as a conceptual founder of quantum computing. It is also why his program is not the second half of von Neumann’s. Feynman derives the two thirds bound himself and never names Bell or Bell’s inequality anywhere in the paper.

The two routes share an origin, a laboratory, a problem, and even a test case in cellular automata. They do not share a premise. Feynman grants the quantum formalism and asks what hardware realizes it. Von Neumann and Ulam do not grant it, and ask what mathematics should replace it. Feynman drew the boundary himself in the discussion following the keynote, correcting a questioner’s vocabulary: “Discretizing is the right word. Quantizing is a different kind of mathematics.” The report therefore treats these as alternative routes chosen by a circle whose members knew each other’s ideas — Feynman, Ulam, von Neumann, Metropolis, Teller and Fermi were the same Los Alamos formation — rather than as a coordinated division of one design. Nothing here suggests either side was unaware of the other.

The short code passage from The Computer and the Brain, quoted earlier, makes mathematics a secondary language built on the primary language of the central nervous system, and makes logic and arithmetic historical accidents rather than necessities. Ulam states the methodological inversion directly: mathematicians “start with axioms whose validity they don’t question,” whereas “in physics the situation is inverse. Given a lot of facts, let’s call them theorems, we look for axioms, that is to say physical laws, from which they would follow.” The discrete route treats physical law as an output of computation. The continuous route treats it as the input.

It is worth being explicit about what kind of machine that implies, because The Computer and the Brain is usually read as a comparison and it is in fact a specification. Von Neumann reports that the nervous system achieves perhaps two or three decimal digits of precision, far below any computing machine of his day, and draws the consequence: “We have seen how this leads to a lower level of arithmetical precision but to a higher level of logical reliability: a deterioration in arithmetics has been traded for an improvement in logics.” He had already warned the 1950 Macy conference that “the underlying mechanism of the nervous system may be best, although somewhat loosely, described as an analogical mechanism,” and that “we do not know at this moment to what extent coded messages are used in the nervous system.” The section of Ulam’s obituary that surveys this work is headed “Theory of automata, probabilistic logic,” and the underlying paper is von Neumann’s Probabilistic Logics and the Synthesis of Reliable Organisms from Unreliable Components. Taken together these describe a hardware architecture that is not the one that was built: low arithmetic precision, high logical reliability, unreliable components, probabilistic rather than deterministic logic, massive parallelism, and a short code rather than a complete one. The machine the discrete route was aiming at was a stochastic machine. That is the architectural counterpart to Monte Carlo, and it is the reason the two halves of von Neumann’s late work — the sampling algorithm and the automaton — are one project rather than two.


### The third route, which nobody called a route

There is a third branch, and it is the one the present day reaches through. Claude Shannon’s 1948 paper made information a measurable quantity with a mathematics of its own, and the measure it arrives at — the sum of probabilities weighted by their logarithms — has the form of the entropy of statistical mechanics. The resemblance was noticed immediately and then set aside as an analogy: suggestive, useful, not to be leaned on. Shannon’s uniqueness theorem is a statement about messages and is indifferent to what carries them, and that indifference is what made the theory portable. It is also why the branch could run for seventy years — coding, compression, estimation, statistical learning, and finally the language model — without ever needing to ask a question about physics.

So the open record after 1948 holds two public programs that do not meet. One is an information science that brackets the physical substrate; the other is a physics of computation in which information is what one reads out at the end. Feynman’s keynote and Shannon’s theorem are each foundational, each heavily published, and each silent on the question the other would have to answer. Neither asks whether the entropy of a physical system and the entropy of a message are the same object or merely similar ones.


### Why von Neumann and Ulam did not divide them

The third route is a route only from outside the circle. Von Neumann and Ulam did not treat information, physical process, computation and living organization as separate subjects, and the evidence for that is in what they built rather than in anything they argued. The Computer and the Brain is not a comparison of a machine to an organ; it is the specification of a machine whose architecture is taken from the organ, stochastic and deliberately imprecise in arithmetic, trading precision for logical reliability. The theory of automata is a theory of computation and of living things in one breath, and Ulam’s own name for it says so: “a combinatorial theory of automata and organisms.” Monte Carlo appears in Ulam’s book not as a numerical device for reactor work but as the general method for problems intractable at any scale, from quantum statistics through “the fundamental processes of replication of a living cell” to astronomy and cosmology. Martin Gardner’s preface names Ulam’s three roads as cellular automata, Monte Carlo and nonlinear processes — three faces of one method, applied indifferently to matter, life and computation.

Rota’s 1985 list is that same unity stated as unfinished business. Theoretical computer science, neurophysiology, molecular biology, psychology and the mathematical theory of information, under a single name, named glamorously. Read as a call for interdisciplinary contact it is unremarkable. Read as what it says — one unit of science, one name — it requires that information and physical process be the same kind of thing.

That is what Rota’s Chapter VIII does, and he states it at the head of the chapter: it will “prove that essentially all the interesting distributions we have seen in probability theory may be defined by entropy considerations.” He closes the section by naming how far that reaches: “there are more distributions determined by maximum entropy, especially those in statistical thermodynamics and quantum mechanics.” And the chapter proves that the entropy in question is unique: five self-evident properties, and one function satisfying them, up to a scale change. The distributions of physics are determined by entropy, and there is one entropy. The entropy of physics and the entropy of information are therefore not two quantities that happen to share an algebraic form. The published literature divides along the same seam the world does: the axiomatic tradition after Shannon fixes the functional and derives no physical distribution, while Jaynes and the maximum entropy tradition assume the functional and obtain distributions by maximising it. Rota taught both halves in one chapter at MIT and submitted neither.


### The division of labor and where it sat

Once the routes are distinguished, the institutional pattern documented elsewhere in this report acquires a sharper shape. The unified route — Monte Carlo, MANIAC, cellular automata, automata theory, Boolean arithmetic, nonlinear computational experiment, and the questions about mind and meaning that grew out of them — developed inside a weapons laboratory, on laboratory machines, among personnel holding clearances, with Rota as its convener, editor, memorialist and Director’s Office representative. The two public routes developed in the open: Feynman’s in an international physics journal without restriction, Shannon’s in the Bell System Technical Journal and everything that followed from it. The asymmetry is visible in the record itself. Feynman’s 1981 keynote is a public conference paper and Shannon’s 1948 paper is among the most cited documents of the century; the equivalent record on Rota’s side is a 1983 Intelligence Community artificial intelligence symposium whose morning session is marked classified secret, a set of classified national security reports, and an NSA Medal for Distinguished Service whose basis the National Academy memoir describes as classified.

The inference is this. On the more probable than not standard, the pattern is better explained by an authorized government interest in the discrete route than by parallel private curiosity. Ulam and Metropolis built it; von Neumann had defined its ambition and died before finishing; Rota organized, interpreted, and carried it into classified forums. Feynman’s route, which accepted the standing physics and proposed new hardware, required no such protection and received none. The government had a continuing interest in one of two known alternatives, and the interest is legible in who held access, who convened the meetings, and which half of the intellectual record went quiet.

Three further facts of record belong beside that. Feynman weighed the discrete option and rejected it on a stated physical ground, that a lattice would make the speed of light depend slightly on direction. Von Neumann had himself abandoned an earlier discrete spacetime model, writing to Ortvay on 17 March 1938 that he considered it “very artificial — and so I still think today”; what persisted was the direction of attack rather than a finished replacement. And Ulam’s “incomplete theoretical skeleton” remark concerns the physicists’ quantum theory rather than von Neumann’s axiomatization of it, which Ulam calls in the same passage “a contribution of absolutely first importance.”


### Understanding as the common question

Feynman’s opening question in The Feynman Lectures was “What do we mean by understanding something?” His gods’ chess game distinguishes knowing fundamental rules from understanding a particular move in a system too complicated to follow. His childhood bird story makes the parallel distinction between possessing names and understanding behavior through observation. Rota and Ulam’s key example reaches the same boundary from the opposite side: even a complete molecular description of a metal object does not disclose its contextual function as a key. The common question is whether formal description, prediction, and naming amount to understanding.

This convergence does not depend on verbal resemblance alone, and it is a convergence of questions rather than of programs. Feynman’s problem concerns whether a representation can reproduce physical behavior; Ulam and Rota’s problem concerns whether a representation can carry contextual meaning. The two routes arrive at the same frontier from opposite premises and define the outer boundaries of the post Manhattan computational inquiry: can a machine simulate what nature does, and can it understand what its representations are about?


## Rota’s mathematical competence: ergodic theory, probability, and entropy

The report’s earlier drafts led with access — appointments, conferences, editorships — and only then with subject matter. That order invites the obvious objection: a well connected professor accumulates affiliations, and affiliations prove nothing. The order is therefore reversed here. Before any institutional argument is made, the question is whether Rota possessed the specific mathematical competence that the discrete route required. He did, and it was his first competence, not a late acquisition.


### Before ergodic theory: Church, logic, and the computation side

The competence begins earlier than the doctorate, and on the other side of the subject. Rota entered Princeton in 1950 and graduated in 1953, and the teacher he writes about at greatest length is Alonzo Church — the author of the lambda calculus, of Church’s thesis on effective calculability, and of the first proof of the undecidability of first order logic. In “Fine Hall in its Golden Age” Rota records that Church’s course in mathematical logic “attracted as many as four students in 1951 (none of them were philosophy students, it must be added, to philosophy’s discredit),” and that when he got stuck, “I went to see Church to discuss my difficulties, and far from giving away the easy solution, he spent hours with me devising direct proofs.”

This matters for two reasons. First, it places Rota inside the computability tradition as an undergraduate, years before the ergodic theory, and hours at a time with the man who defined what a computation is. Whatever else he was, he was not a combinatorialist who wandered into computing questions late. Second, it explains the shape of his competence as this report needs it: logic and effective calculability on one side, ergodic theory and probability on the other. That is precisely the pair the discrete route required — a theory of what machines can compute, and a theory of how to compute by sampling when they cannot. Very few people in his generation held both, and the ones who did are largely the people in this report.


### The first career: operator theory, ergodic theory, probability

Rota took his Yale doctorate in 1956 under Jacob T. Schwartz with a thesis titled Extension theory of differential operators, written inside the group assembling Dunford and Schwartz’s Linear Operators. The thesis is spectral theory and nothing else: in Rota’s own words it addressed “the problem of determining sufficient conditions on non-self-adjoint ordinary differential operators, which will insure that the operators be ‘spectral’ in the sense of Dunford,” and he believed it contained “what is historically the first occurrence of an ‘invariance of index’ theorem.” It was written to a problem his advisor had set: Dunford’s 1958 survey of spectral operators cites Rota’s result on singular operators with rational coefficients and states that such examples “has led Schwartz to conjecture” a general description of the spectrum of singular differential operators, presenting the theory “in the hopes that it will interest others in the problem of verifying the Schwartz conjecture.” The National Academy memoir by Joseph P. S. Kung divides Rota’s work into three periods and describes the first plainly: “In the first period, from the late 1950s through the 1960s, his interests centered on operator theory, differential equations, and ergodic theory,” with papers “on models of operators and invariant subspaces, positive operators, Reynold operators, Frobenius reciprocity and Abel limits in ergodic theory.” Schwartz, writing in the American Mathematical Society memorial, identifies the pivot precisely: “already in his two 1959–60 papers on Reynolds operators, Gianco had stepped onto the bridge of ergodic theory, often a highly combinatorial corner of analysis, that carried him into purer combinatorics.”

Kung records that Rota considered one of his two best research papers to be the 1962 result on sequences of doubly stochastic operators — an almost everywhere convergence theorem proved by constructing an abstract L space, taking a dilation, and applying martingale convergence — published as “An ‘alternierende Verfahren’ for general positive operators,” Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society 68 (1962), pp. 95–102. Doubly stochastic operators, martingales, dilations and ergodic averages are the mathematics of Monte Carlo. This matters for the assessment: the common objection that Rota was a combinatorialist with no stake in stochastic simulation is false at the level of his training. He entered combinatorics from ergodic theory, and he never left probability — the 1998 Fubini lectures were given over to the foundations of probability, and Waterman’s memorial account of Rota’s Los Alamos lecturing lists the topics as “differential equations, ergodic theory, nonstandard analysis, probability, and of course, combinatorics.”

The effect of establishing this first is to change what the institutional record means. A laboratory seeking an interpreter for a program built on Monte Carlo, ergodic behavior, stochastic models and discrete structure was not choosing a famous friend of Ulam’s. It was choosing one of a small number of people trained in exactly that combination.

One further fact about the doctorate belongs here, because it is usually treated as biography rather than as evidence. Rota’s thesis advisor, Jacob T. Schwartz, did not remain a functional analyst. He founded the Department of Computer Science at New York University in 1969 and chaired it until 1977; he served as chairman of the Computer Science Board of the National Research Council; and from November 1987 to September 1989 he directed the Information Science and Technology Office of the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency — the office, renamed from the Information Processing Techniques Office in 1986, through which the United States funded artificial intelligence research. The National Academy of Sciences memorial by Martin Davis and Edmond Schonberg states the consequence without hedging: the directorship “enabled him to seriously influence the direction of research in computer science in the United States.” The New York Times obituary reports what he did with it: “While there he shifted the research focus of the organization away from artificial intelligence.”

The chronology is what carries this, and it is worth stating plainly because it is easy to read 1956 with present day categories. In 1956 there was no computer science. The first computer science department in the United States was founded at Purdue in 1962, six years after Rota defended. What existed instead was the mathematics out of which computing would be built, and that is what this group was doing. Rota learned logic and effective calculability from Church as an undergraduate and took his doctorate in operator theory under Schwartz. Schwartz founded the computer science department at New York University in 1969 and directed DARPA’s Information Science and Technology Office from November 1987 to September 1989. His other doctoral students went, in order: Robert Kupperman in 1962 to United States government national security and crisis management; Stanley Osher in 1966 to computational mathematics; Shmuel Winograd in 1968 to the arithmetic complexity of computations at IBM, where he was already a research staff member and became an IBM Fellow; Ken Kennedy in 1971 to compilers and parallel computing, taking one of the first computer science doctorates NYU awarded; Jerry Hobbs in 1974 to artificial intelligence and natural language at SRI International; Clyde Kruskal in 1981 and Larry Rudolph in 1982 to parallel algorithms and the NYU Ultracomputer. That is what the people did. Those are the placements, in order, as the record gives them.


### 18.313 and the unpublished manuscript

For decades Rota taught 18.313, Introduction to Probability, at MIT. In “Ten Lessons of an MIT Education” he describes it in his own words as “a course I teach in advanced probability theory” that “compresses the material typically taught in a year into one term.” MIT Tech Talk quotes a student calling it “the best probability course at MIT.” The course text was never commercially published. It circulated as a manuscript: Kenneth Baclawski and Gian-Carlo Rota, Introduction to Probability and Random Processes, copyright 1979, a scanned copy of which is available on the Internet Archive; a later expanded version adds Sara Billis as a third author. The 1979 copy appears to be the earliest version publicly available.

The manuscript is evidence in its own right, and of a kind the rest of this report otherwise lacks: a substantial, technically explicit body of Rota’s work that he taught for decades and never published. Its opening states the pedagogical aim in terms that are not those of a combinatorialist: “Probability is one of the great achievements of this century. Like geometry, it is a way of looking at nature. There are many ways of approaching natural problems, many points of view. The geometrical point of view has been with us for thousands of years. The probabilistic point of view is another way of focusing on problems that has been successful in many instances. The purpose of this course is to learn to think probabilistically.” And it names the method at the center of the Los Alamos program without ceremony: “The only technique that we can presently apply to this problem is the ‘method of ignorance’ or the ‘Monte Carlo method’: namely simulate the problem on a computer, and see what happens.”

The failure to publish is the anomaly, and it is measurable against the man’s own reach. Rota took over the Academic Press journal Advances in Mathematics in 1967 and ran it until his death in 1999. He founded Advances in Applied Mathematics in 1980. He was the series editor of the Encyclopedia of Mathematics and Its Applications for Cambridge University Press, whose title pages read “Gian-Carlo Rota, Editor.” For thirty years he decided what appeared in two journals and a Cambridge series. No mathematician of his generation had a shorter path from manuscript to print. His own four hundred page course text never appeared in English. The only printed version is Italian, issued by the Unione Matematica Italiana in Bologna in 1984 with Mauro Cerasoli added as a third author; the later MIT version, circa 1993, adds Sara Billis. The explanation on record is a colleague’s, not Rota’s: David Ellerman, who scanned and posted the 1979 copy, writes that “through the years, Gian-Carlo Rota strove to ‘perfect’ the manuscript until his untimely death in 1999. At that time, only an Italian version had been published.”

The co-author is a fourth instance of the recurrence this report tracks. Kenneth Baclawski took his doctorate at Harvard in 1976 with a dissertation on the homology and combinatorics of ordered sets, squarely in Rota’s combinatorial territory though not under his supervision. His own curriculum vitae records that he was a consultant at Los Alamos National Laboratory from 1979 to 1986 — the period in which the manuscript carrying the entropy theorem was written and taught, and the same years in which Rota was a Los Alamos senior fellow. Baclawski went on to become the sole named inventor on United States Patent 5,694,593, “Distributed computer database system and method,” filed in 1994 and granted in 1997, assigned to Northeastern University: a scheme in which a home node fragments a query, hashes the fragments, distributes them to computer nodes that search local indexes, and gathers and ranks the returned identifiers. Northeastern University and Jarg Corporation, where Baclawski served as chief technology officer and then chief scientist, asserted that patent against Google in the Eastern District of Texas in November 2007; the case settled confidentially and was dismissed with prejudice in April 2011. Baclawski’s faculty page at Northeastern states that in 1993 he “allegedly developed the search engine technology now called MapReduce.” The hedge is his own. So the pattern runs: Rota’s teacher at Princeton defined computability; his doctoral advisor later directed the federal office that funded artificial intelligence; the co-author of his unpublished entropy manuscript consulted at Los Alamos through the years it was written and afterward held the patent at the center of a distributed computation suit against Google. Four people, one laboratory, and the same subject.


### The physics distributions taught as entropy

The report’s earlier framing treated Rota as having no working relationship to quantum mechanics. The course material shows otherwise. Chapter III develops the occupancy model and states the physical reading directly: “In physics the balls are called particles, and the boxes are called states, while a placement is called a configuration or system … In physics, such a restriction is called an exclusion principle: a given state may have at most one particle in it. The physics model we have described here goes by the name of Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics (with or without the exclusion principle). We shall see other statistics in section IV which are more physically realistic.” Maxwell-Boltzmann, Bose-Einstein and Fermi-Dirac statistics are then developed as the three sampling regimes — Bose-Einstein as sampling with replacement, Fermi-Dirac as sampling without — and they appear as indexed topics in the manuscript, not as asides.

Chapter VIII states its own programme in its opening paragraph, and the claim is Rota’s, not this report’s: “That probability is closely connected with information should come as no surprise after problems such as exercise V.5 (the jailer paradox). What entropy does is to make this connection precise. In section 1, we discuss entropy for finite-valued random variables. In the next section we give a dramatic application of the law of large numbers to information theory: the Shannon Coding Theorem. Finally in section 3, we turn to the case of continuous random variables and prove that essentially all the interesting distributions we have seen in probability theory may be defined by entropy considerations.” The distributions “we have seen” are the ones Chapter III had already set out: the occupancy model, particles and states, the exclusion principle, Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics, and Bose-Einstein and Fermi-Dirac statistics as sampling with and without replacement.

He carries it out and then says how far it reaches. The section summary: “The four principal processes of probability theory are all determined by maximum entropy properties.” He constructs discrete entropy, relative entropy, Boltzmann or differential entropy, and standard entropy — “in order to speak of the entropy of the phenomenon represented by a random variable, independent of scale changes … the standard entropy of a random variable is the Boltzmann entropy of its standardization” — and characterises each distribution as a maximiser of entropy under a constraint: the uniform on an interval, the exponential among positive variables of given mean, and the normal as the unique maximiser of standard entropy. Then the closing remark of the section, which states the scope in his own voice: “There are more distributions determined by maximum entropy, especially those in statistical thermodynamics and quantum mechanics, but I trust that you now see the basic ideas.” The distributions of physics are not imported from physics and used here. They are named as determined by entropy, in a probability course, in a manuscript that was never published.


### The uniqueness of entropy, and the coding theorem

The chapter proves a uniqueness theorem. Rota raises it as a question about definition and answers it: “Although these examples provide some motivation for our definition of entropy, they leave unanswered the more difficult question of why, out of all possible definitions, we use this one. We will do this by finding five self-evident properties that ought to hold for any reasonable measure of information (or entropy). It then turns out that our definition of entropy is the only one that satisfies all these properties.” The result as the text states it: “if H satisfies the above five properties, then H is given by the formula introduced earlier in this chapter, except for a possible scale change.” Accept the five properties and there is exactly one entropy formula. Not a preferred one, not a conventional one — one.

The five properties are stated as an identification test: entropy is a function on sets of non-negative reals summing to one; it is unaffected by adjoining a zero probability; it is continuous; for a refinement, the conditional entropy equals the difference of entropies, H(pi|sigma) = H(pi) − H(sigma); and the uniform distribution maximises it. Rota then states the uniqueness result: “We are now ready for the following remarkable fact: if H satisfies the above five properties, then H is given by the formula introduced earlier in this chapter, except for a possible scale change.” Formally: if H satisfies the five properties then there is a constant C with H(p1, …, pn) = C times the sum of pi log base two of one over pi. Of the proof Rota remarks, characteristically, “The proof is rather technical, so we suggest omitting it on the first reading.”

The chapter is bookended by that theorem and by the Shannon Coding Theorem, which Rota presents not as a result imported from communication engineering but as “a dramatic application of the law of large numbers to information theory.” So Chapter VIII opens by making entropy the thing that defines the distributions, including, in his own words, those of statistical thermodynamics and quantum mechanics; proves that exactly one entropy function exists; and reaches the founding theorem of information theory as a consequence of the law of large numbers. Taught annually at MIT. Submitted nowhere.


### What the theorem does for the program

This is where the manuscript stops being a curiosity about an unpublished textbook and becomes the centrally relevant evidence in this report, because it supplies the one thing the Los Alamos program was built on and had never been given: a proof that its method must work.

The working practice of the discrete route was to compute continuous physical quantities by sampling discrete random histories on a machine. It worked. Nobody had shown that it had to. The justification offered at the time was frankly pragmatic — Ulam, in the 1958 memorial, defends it as heuristic: the differential equations “are not, so to say, very sacrosanct any more,” and “a direct study of models of the system on computing machines may possess a heuristic value, at least.” That is an admission, not a foundation. Monte Carlo was a technique that succeeded empirically while the question of why a discrete sampling process should reproduce a continuum distribution at all was left open. The same gap is visible at the other end of the subject. Von Neumann to Shannon, on naming the quantity: “no one really knows what entropy really is.” Rota in print, on the principle the whole practice rests on: “The maximum entropy principle is one of the hot potatoes of our day … no one has yet succeeded in finding a justification for it. Maybe we should make it one of the axioms of statistics.” And Rota at Los Alamos, as recorded in the volume of remembrances: “Out of all of mathematics the thing he most wanted to understand was the maximum entropy principle. ‘No-one really knows why it works,’ he said.”

Chapter VIII closes that gap. If the continuous physical distribution simply is a scaled discrete entropy, then there exists a discrete probability structure whose entropy generates it, and the continuum description is a re-encoding of a discrete one rather than a thing of a different kind. The consequences for the program are immediate and they are the reason this belongs at the center of the report rather than in an appendix. First, discrete sampling is not an approximation scheme that happens to converge on the right answer; it is the natural computation, operating on the structure that was actually there. Second, the success of Monte Carlo is thereby guaranteed rather than observed — the theorem is an existence proof that the physics is computable, and therefore that the method must work. Third, it retires the objection that the discrete route was a numerical convenience adopted because the analysis was hard. On this result the discrete description is prior and the continuum is the derived object, which is precisely what von Neumann was asserting when he told Birkhoff he no longer believed absolutely in Hilbert space, what Ulam meant by saying Monte Carlo “reverses the treatment” of classical probability theory, and what Rota meant when he wrote that a sample space “should be brought into existence for amplitudes and quantum mechanics should be developed starting from this concept.”

The evidentiary force of this is independent of any inference about classification. It is a positive, checkable exhibit. The theorem that supplies the foundation of the government’s computational program sits in a four hundred page manuscript, taught at MIT for roughly two decades by a man who spent several weeks of every year at Los Alamos, and it never entered the literature. The inversion within his own corpus is the part a jury can check in an afternoon: the published Rota says the justification for the maximum entropy principle has never been found; the unpublished Rota taught the machinery every spring. On scope: this report assesses the documentary record, and the mathematics of Chapter VIII is reported as it stands in the manuscript, with Rota’s own statements of the properties and of the uniqueness result quoted directly. Questions of proof and of priority belong in a mathematical paper. The record here is that the result was written, was taught at MIT for roughly two decades, and was never published.


### Probability and quantum mechanics

Rota’s relation to quantum mechanics is explicit in his own writing, and it is the relation this report’s thesis would predict: not the physicist’s, but the probabilist’s. In his list of problems he would never solve he writes that behind the Feynman integral “there lurks an even more enticing (and even less rigorous) concept: that of an amplitude which is meant to be the quantum-mechanical analog of probability (one gets probabilities by taking the absolute values of amplitudes and squaring them: hence the slogan ‘quantum mechanics is the imaginary square root of probability theory’). A concept similar to that of a sample space should be brought into existence for amplitudes and quantum mechanics should be developed starting from this concept.”

That is a program, not an aphorism, and it is the discrete route’s program: do not take the quantum formalism as given and build hardware to obey it; build the probabilistic foundation from which the formalism should have been derived. It is the same move von Neumann made when he told Birkhoff he no longer believed absolutely in Hilbert space, and the same one Ulam recorded as von Neumann’s late ambition. Rota states elsewhere, in the MIT Tech Talk interview, how he understood the historical relationship of his own field to physics: asked what problems combinatorics had solved in the past, he answered, “One example is quantum mechanics, which was discovered 30 years ago. The mathematics behind quantum mechanics had been worked out 20 years before by a mathematician who didn’t know what it was good for.”


## Direction and descent, 1954 to 1957

The question the institutional argument leaves open is why a government would sponsor one of two intellectual routes. The answer available on the public record is that for seventeen years the man who defined the discrete route also sat at the center of the machinery that set the direction of American military research, and that Ulam printed the roster. From the obituary: member of the Scientific Advisory Committee, Ballistic Research Laboratories, Aberdeen, 1940–1957; Navy Bureau of Ordnance 1941–1955; consultant to Los Alamos 1943–1955; Naval Ordnance Laboratory 1947–1955; member of the Research and Development Board 1949–1953; consultant to Oak Ridge 1949–1954; Armed Forces Special Weapons Project 1950–1955; Scientific Advisory Board of the United States Air Force 1951–1957; the General Advisory Committee by presidential appointment 1952–1954; the Technical Advisory Panel on Atomic Energy 1953–1957; and Chairman of the Advisory Committee on Guided Missiles 1954–1957. In October 1954 he was nominated by the President to the United States Atomic Energy Commission; the Senate did not act that year, and he took office on 15 March 1955. Ulam records what he gave up to take it: “He left Princeton on a leave of absence and discontinued all commitments with the exception of the chairmanship of the ICBM Committee.” Of everything he held, the missile chairmanship is the one he kept.

What he did with the Commission seat is stated by Ulam directly, and it is the single most important sentence in this section because it supplies a mechanism rather than an inference: “When in 1954 he became a member of the Atomic Energy Commission, he worked on the technical and economical problems relating to the building and operation of fission reactors. In this position he also spent a great deal of time in the organization of studies of mathematical computing machines and the means to make them available to universities and other research centers.” The report therefore does not need to speculate that von Neumann used federal authority to direct computing research. His closest collaborator says in the official memorial that he did.

The simultaneity is the point. The years in which von Neumann held the Commission seat and the missile chairmanship are the same years in which he was writing The Computer and the Brain, which he never finished, and in which the Los Alamos group around him was running the Fermi Pasta Ulam Tsingou experiment on MANIAC. The automata manuscript begun in the Illinois lectures of 1949 was also unfinished at his death and was completed editorially by Burks only in 1966. A sitting federal commissioner, responsible for the direction of atomic research and chairing the committee that redirected the national missile program, spent his last years writing an unpublished account of a machine architecture that did not exist. That is an unusual allocation of a very scarce man’s attention, and it is not explained by hobby.

The descent is then a matter of who stayed. Von Neumann died in February 1957. Ulam — by every account including his own the closest of his American friendships, and the man to whom von Neumann’s obituary was entrusted — remained at Los Alamos, having already supplied the cellular framework, the Monte Carlo method and the nonlinear computational experiment. Metropolis remained and built the MANIAC series. Teller remained and is a co-author of the Metropolis algorithm paper. Fermi had died in 1954 but the sampling method he began in Rome was by then the laboratory’s working practice. Rota arrived in the late nineteen sixties, became a senior fellow in 1971, and spent several weeks a year there for the rest of his life, editing Metropolis on the history of computing, editing Ulam, memorializing Ulam, and eventually representing the Director’s Office at an Intelligence Community symposium on artificial intelligence. Feynman, from the same formation, went to Caltech and published the alternative route in an international journal without restriction.

One detail of the chronology is usually read backwards. Von Neumann was confirmed to the Commission in March 1955 and Lewis Strauss, quoted by Ulam, bounds his period of full function: “During the period between the date of his confirmation and the late autumn, 1955, Johnny functioned magnificently.” That is a short tenure, and it is short because the illness was found that summer. But the program he brought to the seat was not conceived there. He delivered the Hixon Symposium paper on the general and logical theory of automata in September 1948, within three years of the Los Alamos work, and lectured on the theory of self reproducing automata at Illinois in December 1949. By the time of the presidential appointment in October 1954 he had been developing the automata program for six years. He arrived at the Commission with it already formulated, and Ulam records what he then did there: he “spent a great deal of time in the organization of studies of mathematical computing machines and the means to make them available to universities and other research centers.” The succession argument likewise rests on content rather than affiliation: the people who remained did discrete, stochastic and automata work, and the one who left did continuous field quantum work, and each of those is checkable independently of where anybody was employed.


## The new science that remains to be named

The single most direct evidence of what Rota thought he was doing is a passage the report had not used. In the 1985 Los Alamos Science dialogue with David Sharp — published by the laboratory, while Rota was a seventeen year consultant with Director’s Office access — Sharp describes the neural network lineage from McCulloch and Pitts through von Neumann, Kleene and Shannon, and Rota answers:

“As you were saying, a new unit in science is being formed that remains to be named. It will include the best of theoretical computer science, neurophysiology, molecular biology, psychology, and the mathematical theory of information. It will be important to name it properly. As the Latin proverb says, ‘Nomen est omen.’” Sharp: “The name is a presage.” Rota: “We need to name it glamorously, to make it into a respectable profession, and to bring people together to work on it. This is beginning to happen. It is the central drama of today’s science.”

Set that beside Ulam’s description of what von Neumann had been attempting when he died. Ulam: “it was toward the end of his life that he felt sure enough of himself to engage freely and yet painstakingly in the creation of a possible new mathematical discipline. This was to be a combinatorial theory of automata and organisms.” Rota, twenty seven years later, at the same laboratory: a new unit in science, not yet named, combining computation, neurophysiology, molecular biology, psychology and information theory. The two descriptions are the same description. The disciplines Rota lists are the disciplines von Neumann’s theory of automata was assembled from, and Ulam names the same ingredients in the obituary: “Its notions and terminology come from several fields — mathematics, electrical engineering, and neurology.”

Three features of the passage bear on the assessment. First, Rota is not surveying someone else’s field; he uses the first person plural of a participant — “We need to name it … and to bring people together to work on it” — and calls it “the central drama of today’s science.” Second, the thing he describes has no name, which is to say it has no journals, no society, no conference circuit and no publication record; a field without a name is a field whose work cannot be tracked bibliographically. Third, none of it appears in his own academic publications. His bibliography contains combinatorics, invariant theory, probability and philosophy. It contains no papers in the field he describes as the central drama of contemporary science and says he is trying to assemble people to work on. The same volume of remembrances puts the point about naming in a plainer register, of the sequence alignment and molecular evolution work he and Waterman began at Los Alamos: “It was not called computational biology then. It was not called anything at all.”

This is the report’s strongest single line of argument and it stands on public texts alone. Three of them, read together: Ulam saying in 1958 what von Neumann was building, Rota saying in 1985 that he is building the same thing and that it needs a name, and Rota’s own bibliography, which is silent on it.


## Rota as convener curator and government intermediary

**Established facts**

Rota’s relationship with Los Alamos was long, recurring, and unusually close to senior leadership. The National Academy memoir states that he was a senior fellow from 1971 and spent several weeks at the laboratory every year. Los Alamos Science described him in 1985 as a consultant of seventeen years and printed his deliberately informal description of entering the Director’s office to talk. The CIA’s 1983 symposium program used the more specific title Director’s Office Fellow. These records make a purely ceremonial relationship improbable.


### History of computing conference

The International Research Conference on the History of Computing met at Los Alamos Scientific Laboratory from 10 to 15 June 1976. Nicholas Metropolis, Jack Howlett, and Rota edited the resulting volume, A History of Computing in the Twentieth Century. The proceedings brought pioneers and historians together around machine architecture, programming, wartime computing, and institutional memory. Rota’s editorial role placed him beside Metropolis in selecting, preserving, and framing the history of a field in which Los Alamos had both public and classified stakes.

Conferences define genealogies, connect generations, and identify questions worth preserving. The evidence shows Rota doing that work.


### Science and the information onslaught

This conference is more probative than the report’s earlier drafts made it, and the primary document settles what it was. Science, Computers, and the Information Onslaught, Academic Press, 1984, is the proceedings of a meeting held at Los Alamos in June 1981. The editors are Donald M. Kerr, Karl Braithwaite, N. Metropolis, David H. Sharp and Gian-Carlo Rota; Rota is listed in the contributors as “Massachusetts Institute of Technology … and Los Alamos National Laboratory,” and he is a co-author of the concluding paper as well as a co-editor. The editors’ preface states the occasion of the meeting in terms that are not ambiguous: “National means of surveillance provide intelligence services with vastly larger quantities of information, well beyond the capacity of the available cadre of trained personnel to analyze,” and the meeting brought together “distinguished representatives from the sciences of mathematics, physics, linguistics, and computers … with leaders in national security, science administration, and government.”

The contributor list is the evidence. It includes Louis W. Tordella, listed with the National Security Agency, Fort George G. Meade — for sixteen years the Agency’s Deputy Director, the longest serving in its history — and Noel A. M. Gayler, a former Director of the National Security Agency. It includes Edward Teller; Richard L. Garwin; William J. Perry, until shortly before the meeting the Under Secretary of Defense for Research and Engineering and later Secretary of Defense; Lowell L. Wood of Lawrence Livermore; Morgan Sparks and Howard Stump of Sandia; and Donald M. Kerr, then Director of Los Alamos and later Director of the National Reconnaissance Office and Principal Deputy Director of National Intelligence. It also includes Jacob T. Schwartz of the Courant Institute, contributing a chapter titled “Large-Scale Parallel Computers.”

Two things follow. First, this is not a general academic meeting at which a professor happened to speak. It is a Los Alamos conference on the analysis of intelligence collection, attended by the serving and former leadership of the National Security Agency, the Director of the weapons laboratory, and a former Under Secretary of Defense, and Rota is one of its five editors. That is a stronger fact than the Director’s Office title and it has a published document behind it. Second, it places Rota and his own doctoral advisor in the same room and the same volume, in 1981, on the subject of large scale computation for national information problems, six years before Schwartz went to DARPA.

The conference belonged to a weapons laboratory led by Kerr. Its subject was the analysis of what national means of surveillance were collecting. Its contributors included the National Security Agency’s longest serving Deputy Director, a former Director of that Agency, a former Under Secretary of Defense, and the Director of the laboratory. Its proceedings were published by Academic Press and are open. Rota is named on the volume as one of five editors, and of Los Alamos.


### Ulam memorial and the Barrier of Meaning

Rota spoke twice at Los Alamos in Ulam’s memory. At the memorial service at The Lodge on 17 May 1984 he said of Ulam: “His word then became the warning of the prophet, the mumbled riddle of the Sybil in her trance.” A year later he opened the laboratory’s conference Evolution, Games and Learning, 20–25 May 1985, with “The Barrier of Meaning,” published in Letters in Mathematical Physics 10 (1985) and reprinted as “In Memoriam of Stan Ulam: The Barrier of Meaning” in Physica D 22 (1986), where it opened the special issue associated with the conference volume. The essay recalls Ulam asking whether the connective “as” could be formalized: the difference between processing symbols and taking something as something meaningful. Rota closes by asking whether AI could cross that barrier.

The memorial context is important. Rota did not introduce the problem in an abstract AI survey. He presented it through Ulam, at the institution where Ulam had worked, to an audience connected to a meeting on evolution, games, and learning. The framing joined institutional memory, foundational questions about intelligent behavior, and fields central to later complexity research.

Rota’s description of himself as Ulam’s hermeneut supplies the functional name for his role. A hermeneut interprets fragmentary, compressed, or context dependent thought for another audience. Rota preserved Ulam’s conversations, elicited explanations of von Neumann and Feynman, translated intuitions into mathematical and philosophical problems, edited the history of Los Alamos computation, and placed the barrier of meaning inside an AI and complexity setting. The term describes organized intellectual work, not passive friendship.


### Mathematics philosophy and artificial intelligence

The 1985 Los Alamos Science dialogue with Rota and David Sharp is direct evidence of Rota’s public AI engagement. Rota described an emerging field combining theoretical computer science, neurophysiology, molecular biology, psychology, and information theory, and emphasized the need to name the field and bring people together to work on it. The statement is a description of intellectual institution building, made in the laboratory’s own periodical, in the year he opened its conference on evolution, games and learning.

### The positions and what they carried

Alongside the roles, the offices. Rota was founding editor of the Journal of Combinatorial Theory in 1966 with Frank Harary; he took over Advances in Mathematics in 1967, on the condition, his publisher records, of “giving him complete editorial license,” and held it until his death; he founded Advances in Applied Mathematics, whose first volume appeared in 1980; and he was editor of the Encyclopedia of Mathematics and Its Applications from its first volume in 1976, the series beginning at Addison-Wesley and moving to Cambridge University Press later. He founded two book series, Mathematicians of Our Time at MIT Press and Contemporary Mathematicians at Birkhäuser, and edited the Advances in Mathematics Supplementary Studies volumes. He sat on the editorial boards of the Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society, Discrete Mathematics, Studies in Applied Mathematics, the European Journal of Combinatorics, the Journal of Mathematical Analysis and Applications, the SIAM Journal on Applied Mathematics, the Proceedings of the Royal Society of Edinburgh, and, from 1996, the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. He also wrote the book review section of Advances in Mathematics himself. An editor of a journal sees submitted work before the field does, including the work he declines.

At MIT he supervised between forty two and forty nine doctoral students, depending on which list is used, from 1960 to 1998, and he placed them. The American Mathematical Society memorial records the mechanism in his own words. Of W. Y. C. Chen, who took his doctorate in 1991 and then spent five years as a research fellow at Los Alamos: “Go to Los Alamos and look up Jim Louck.” Michael Hawrylycz, doctorate 1994, went to the Computer Research and Applications Group at Los Alamos. The same memorial names two MIT physics undergraduates, Mark Ettinger and David McComas, who went to the laboratory because of Rota; McComas later directed the Center for Space Science and Exploration.

His curriculum vitae states the access directly, under its own headings. Under Consultant: “Los Alamos Scientific Laboratory, 1966–. The Rand Corporation, 1966–1971. Brookhaven National Laboratory, 1969–1973.” Under Honors: “Director’s Office Fellow, Los Alamos National Laboratory, 1971–.” Under a heading he saw no reason to leave out of a public document, Security Clearances: “Top Secret Clearance (Air Force) 1969–1971. Q – Clearance (DOE), 1966–. SI – Clearance (NSA), 1981–.” Under Administrative positions: the Office of Naval Research mathematics advisory committee, 1963 to 1967; the Air Force Office of Scientific Research Directorate Evaluation Panel, 1971 to 1974; the Institute for Defense Analyses Focus Committee, 1985 to 1988; the Santa Fe Institute trusteeship, 1985 to 1990; the DIMACS Board of Advisors, 1990 to 1993; and the chairmanship of Section 11, mathematics, of the National Academy of Sciences, 1994 to 1997.

Set out together: a Department of Energy Q clearance from 1966 and a National Security Agency special intelligence clearance from 1981, both running to the end of his life; a standing Director’s Office fellowship at the laboratory from 1971; seats on Navy, Air Force and Institute for Defense Analyses review panels across twenty five years; the editorship of the journals in which new work in discrete mathematics, combinatorics and applied mathematics arrived for judgment; and a doctoral pipeline at MIT that he directed by name toward Los Alamos. Those are the positions and their dates, as he stated them.


## The classified record


### The 1983 Intelligence Community AI Symposium

Two declassified documents govern this event and should be cited together. The first is the official program, CIA-RDP85-00142R000100030008-2, whose cover reads: “ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE SYMPOSIUM — ‘INTELLIGENCE APPLICATIONS OF AI’ — Sponsored By U.S. INTELLIGENCE COMMUNITY — December 6, 7, & 8, 1983 — MAIN AUDITORIUM, CIA HEADQUARTERS, LANGLEY, VA. — By Invitation Only.” Two of the six sessions carry the marking “THIS SESSION WILL BE CLASSIFIED SECRET,” both on the second day, Wednesday 7 December; no such marking appears on the first or third day sessions. Rota is listed in the second day morning session, at 9:15, as “Dr. Gian-Carlo Rota, Director’s Office Fellow, Los Alamos National Laboratories, Professor of Applied Mathematics and Philosophy, MIT,” speaking on “Observations and Speculations on the Effect of the Computer on Scientists.”

The second document is the symposium summary report, CIA-RDP86M00886R000500040010-5, transmitted 12 July 1984 by Philip K. Eckman, Chairman of the AI Steering Group, to the Executive Director of Central Intelligence. It confirms the sponsoring body — “the Artificial Intelligence Steering Group (AISG) of the Intelligence Research and Development Council sponsored the second Annual Symposium on Intelligence Applications of AI” — the scale, “Over 600 people attended last year’s Symposium,” the venue, “the Tunnel adjacent to the main Auditorium at CIA Headquarters,” and the classification: “These presentations were made during the second day of the Symposium, in sessions classified at the Secret level. In order to facilitate distribution, this report is to be kept unclassified.”

Set out plainly, this is what the two documents establish. The symposium was convened by an Intelligence Community body, the Artificial Intelligence Steering Group of the Intelligence Research and Development Council. It was held in the main auditorium at CIA Headquarters and was by invitation only, and more than six hundred people were invited. Two of its six sessions were designated Secret, both falling on the second day. Rota was there in the laboratory’s own capacity and under its own title, Director’s Office Fellow, Los Alamos National Laboratories, and he was scheduled on that second day. By then he had held a Department of Energy Q clearance for seventeen years and an NSA special intelligence clearance for two. And the proceedings were reduced to a summary report which the Chairman of the AI Steering Group transmitted to the Executive Director of Central Intelligence, and which was deliberately kept unclassified so that it could be circulated inside the community.

That last fact identifies what kind of event this was. Six hundred invited people, convened by an intelligence body, whose product is an internal summary sent to the Executive Director of Central Intelligence, is the shape of a collection exercise rather than a briefing: the government is not disclosing to the attendees, it is assembling them, hearing them, and distributing what it learned. Rota had co-edited a volume out of precisely that arrangement two years earlier, and its preface states the problem it was convened to address — that national means of surveillance were producing information “well beyond the capacity of the available cadre of trained personnel to analyze” — and describes the remedy as bringing scientists together “with leaders in national security, science administration, and government.” A Director’s Office Fellow at a forum of that design is there in an acquisition capacity, and what he contributed from a lectern is the smallest part of what he was doing in the building.

Against that, the summary report should be quoted for what it does show. Rota’s remarks are summarized at section 2.2.4, within the general “Overview of AI Research and Applications,” rather than at section 2.3, the section drawn from the Secret second day sessions. The report records his substance: “Dr. Rota observed that in computer chess, the winning programs have proved to be those that employ brute force search rather than relying on ingenious heuristics. ‘It’s a sad commentary on the human condition that reliability wins over genius!’” and that in his view two new frontiers for AI were “transition from digital to holographic computation” and “the unexpected relevance of the theory of random nets and non-deterministic programming to AI computation.”

That placement constrains one claim and one only: it is evidence that the content of his own podium remarks was not classified. It is not evidence about his role, and it should not be read as though it were. A cleared man addressing six hundred invited people says nothing classified from the lectern, because that is what the clearance obliges; the unclassified character of his public remarks is the expected conduct of a man holding compartment clearances, not a sign that he held none. The document says as much about itself — it was “to be kept unclassified” precisely so that it could circulate, which is an announcement that it is not a full account of the symposium. What a man with an NSA special intelligence clearance was doing at an Intelligence Community symposium on the applications of artificial intelligence is not recoverable from an unclassified summary of his time on a podium, and being listed as a speaker may be the least of it.

The record therefore shows: the Intelligence Community convened a symposium on the applications of artificial intelligence at CIA Headquarters, part of it classified; Rota attended under the laboratory’s own title, holding a Q clearance and an NSA special intelligence clearance; he was scheduled on the day the classified sessions fell; the forum’s own product was an internal summary sent to the Executive Director of Central Intelligence; and the unclassified summary of his own remarks records what could be said in public and nothing beyond it. One correction to earlier accounts should be noted: peer reviewed scholarship has placed this symposium at Defense Intelligence Agency headquarters. Both primary documents place it at CIA Headquarters, Langley. Defense Intelligence Agency personnel moderated other sessions, which may be the origin of the error.

Four things follow. Rota represented Los Alamos in an Intelligence Community AI event. His scheduled session block was designated Secret. His talk sat among operational defense and intelligence applications. And his public title understates neither the government setting nor his proximity to laboratory leadership — “Director’s Office Fellow” is the laboratory’s own designation, printed in a government program, and it is a more specific claim than the “consultant” of the open literature.


### Classified reports and the NSA medal

Michael Waterman’s contribution to the 2000 American Mathematical Society memorial issue states that Rota served on Los Alamos’s Advisory Board for Nonproliferation and International Security and wrote classified reports on national security issues. The National Academy memoir independently records the 1992 NSA Medal for Distinguished Service and says the work that earned it was classified. These sources establish a nontrivial classified portfolio. They also demonstrate why an open bibliography cannot be treated as a complete account of Rota’s professional activity.


### Disposing of the cryptologic explanation

The obvious alternative reading of the NSA medal is cryptanalysis. A distinguished combinatorialist and probabilist is exactly the sort of consultant the National Security Agency retains for cryptologic mathematics, and that explanation requires no thesis about Los Alamos, automata or artificial intelligence. The objection deserves to be stated in its strongest form and answered, because a reader who supplies it unaided will conclude the report did not think of it.

The answer is that the cryptologic hypothesis explains one item out of at least four, and the four are institutionally unrelated. The NSA medal is an NSA matter. The Director’s Office Fellowship is a Los Alamos National Laboratory title with no NSA connection and no cryptologic content. The Advisory Board for Nonproliferation and International Security concerns fissile material, treaty verification and weapons proliferation, which is not cryptanalysis. The 1983 symposium is a third institution again — an Intelligence Community forum on applications of artificial intelligence, sponsored by the AI Steering Group of the Intelligence Research and Development Council, at which Rota appears under his Los Alamos title. A cryptologic career would produce the first of these and none of the other three. The pattern to be explained is the conjunction, and cryptanalysis does not explain the conjunction.

Two further observations bear on it. First, Rota’s one visible technical collaboration in this area is with Nicholas Metropolis on the Boolean analysis of binary addition and multiplication and on carry propagation as a route to computational complexity — Los Alamos computing, not signals intelligence. Second, the National Academy memoir states that the basis of the medal was classified; that is what the public record establishes about it. The cryptologic reading, offered as a complete alternative explanation of Rota’s government work, is underdetermined by the evidence it purports to explain.

The classified reports, the substance of Rota’s intelligence presentation, and the basis of the NSA medal are the records most likely to state his assignments directly. Their absence is not random archival silence. Independent evidence establishes why they cannot be inspected. What remains visible is a pattern running three decades: authorized access renewed by three agencies, technical and organizational conduct in the same subjects, and classified recognition at the end of it.


### Institutional continuity under classification

The Atomic Energy Act defines Restricted Data to include information concerning the design, manufacture, or use of atomic weapons unless declassified or removed from that category. Department of Energy histories explain the unusual “born secret” feature of nuclear information and the scale of the resulting classified archive. The Progressive litigation in 1979 showed the government willing to seek prior restraint against a magazine article synthesizing public sources about hydrogen bomb design. The district court initially accepted the government’s argument that compilation of public information could acquire a dangerous classified character.

This legal and administrative setting makes continued classified work on weapons related computation independently probable and explains why its deepest extensions would leave an incomplete public bibliography. The alternative theory requires Rota to have accumulated Director’s Office access, private technical briefings, high level political involvement, classified intelligence participation, classified reports, and classified NSA recognition by a succession of unrelated accidents. That is the alternative, stated in full, against the legal and subject matter setting described above.


### The documented concealment mechanism

The concealment mechanism itself is documented in the official history and does not require a modern interpreter. Henry DeWolf Smyth’s Atomic Energy for Military Purposes records that the National Research Council’s Reference Committee, chaired by Gregory Breit, controlled publication policy in fields of possible military interest, and that journal editors referred doubtful papers to it for review. That is the historical signature the present analysis relies on: a government may remove a technical field from ordinary publication while leaving its personnel publicly visible as professors. The expected evidentiary trace is then indirect — anomalous concentrations of specialists, redirected or absent publication, institutional access, and patterns that visible academic careers do not explain. A version of this argument was put in popular form by Eric Weinstein in a 2026 All In interview; the report cites Smyth directly rather than the interview, because the mechanism is established by the official record and an argument that leans on a podcast invites dismissal by association.

Applied to Rota, the expected signature is present. His open technical collaborations reach binary representation, carry propagation, Boolean algorithms and computational complexity, and they stop. His later visible work moves toward conferences, histories, dialogues and philosophical exposition, while direct evidence simultaneously places his national security work behind classification. The unpublished probability manuscript belongs here too: a four hundred page technical text, taught for two decades, containing a uniqueness theorem for entropy, that never entered the literature at all.

### The threshold that was never crossed

Circulation was never the issue. Rota circulated the probability manuscript: roughly four hundred pages, given to MIT students term after term in 18.313, photocopied, carried out of the room and passed on. On any ordinary use of the word that is publishing, in the same sense in which printing a few hundred copies in Bologna was publishing. He was getting the word out, and the manuscript survives today because he did.

The threshold that matters here is narrower, and it is the one already named in this report as the falsification test. It is publication in a peer reviewed journal. That is the act that puts a method into the permanent, citable, reusable scientific record, that invites specialists to check it and build on it, and that a classification obligation reaches.

Few mathematicians of the century had an easier path across it. Rota was editor of Advances in Mathematics from 1967 until his death in 1999, founding editor of Advances in Applied Mathematics in 1980, and series editor of the Cambridge University Press Encyclopedia of Mathematics and Its Applications. He held the gate. He was among the most cited combinatorialists alive and could place work essentially at will. For him, submission was not a gamble.

Not one of the proofs in the four hundred pages went that route. Chapter VIII alone contains a uniqueness theorem for entropy — five stated properties, one functional, up to a scale change — together with the claim, in Rota’s own words, that the distributions of statistical thermodynamics and quantum mechanics are among those “determined by maximum entropy.” That is short, self contained, and by the ordinary standards of the literature straightforwardly publishable. It was taught for years and submitted nowhere.

So the line Rota observed was not between keeping the material quiet and letting it out. He let it out, to anyone who enrolled. The line ran between teaching it and entering it into the reviewed literature, and he stayed on one side of that line for the rest of his life.


### A testable exhibit: the year by year bibliography

The claim is now shown rather than asserted. The exhibit below is a year by year count of every Rota publication located from 1956 to 1999, classified under a criterion fixed in advance and applied by researchers who were given the criterion and not the argument: Category A, technical research, states a method, proof, theorem or algorithm in a form another specialist can test and reuse; Category B, exposition, history, editorial matter, review, memorial or philosophy, discusses a field without supplying a reusable technique; Category C, taught or circulated but never formally published. What changed was not the volume of technical work. It was the subject.

Six of those items belong to computation: the two Significance Arithmetic papers of 1973 and 1974, The Real Numbers as a Wreath Product in 1975, A Boolean Analysis of Addition and Multiplication in 1977, and the two papers on the faces and the lattice of faces of the n cube in 1978. In the twenty one years that follow, across fifty nine technical papers — nine of them in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences — there is not one paper on computation, machine architecture, algorithms for physical simulation, or quantum mechanical method. The technical output moves wholly into invariant theory, superalgebra, umbral calculus, combinatorics and geometric probability, and stays there until he dies.

Meanwhile the other column moves. He did not go quiet and he did not stop proving things. He went on proving things in every field except the one that overlapped his government work, and in that one field he wrote for the public and taught behind a classroom door. This is the exhibit stated as a falsifiable claim: a single peer reviewed Rota paper after 1978 developing computational technique, machine architecture, simulation algorithm or quantum mechanical method defeats it. ### Ulam’s open record

Ulam’s record shows a similar division, although he did publish technical work on Monte Carlo, nonlinear problems, mathematics, physics, and biology. His books and essays, including Science Computers and People and autobiographical or historical pieces, range widely over AI, heuristics, games, evolution, and the use of random methods on difficult problems. His public prose often sketches possibilities instead of presenting a complete technical program.

What Ulam’s own volume makes explicit is the intended scope of the method. Martin Gardner’s preface to Science, Computers, and People lists the three roads as “Cellular automata theory, which he proposed to von Neumann; the Monte Carlo method of solving intractable problems, not only in probability theory but in areas such as number theory where the method would not have been thought applicable; and his work on nonlinear processes that anticipated today’s interest in solitons and ‘chaos’.” The volume itself ranges from quantum statistics and the indistinguishability of particles, through nonlinear dynamics and the growth of patterns, to molecular biology — “the fundamental processes of replication of a living cell, the code describing the construction of materials necessary for what we call life” — and out to astronomical and cosmological problems. Monte Carlo is not presented there as a numerical trick for reactor design. It is presented as the general method for problems that are computationally intractable at any scale, from the quantum to the biological to the astrophysical. That is a claim about what computation is for, and it is the same claim the cellular automaton makes in a different register. Gardner’s preface, incidentally, is also the source of the line about the H bomb that is frequently misattributed to Rota’s editorial introduction: “We still do not know — it remains a government secret — what jumped into Ulam’s head that made it possible for Edward Teller to build an H-bomb.”

Ulam’s 1958 memorial essay for von Neumann is the source commonly associated with the technological “singularity.” Ulam writes: “One conversation centered on the ever accelerating progress of technology and changes in the mode of human life, which gives the appearance of approaching some essential singularity in the history of the race beyond which human affairs, as we know them, could not continue.”


### United States v The Progressive

In 1979 the United States sought to stop The Progressive from publishing Howard Morland’s article on hydrogen bomb design. The government relied on the Atomic Energy Act and argued that an unclassified synthesis of public materials could constitute Restricted Data and cause grave harm. The district court issued a preliminary injunction. The case later became moot after comparable information appeared elsewhere, so it produced no definitive appellate resolution of the constitutional issue.

The case is relevant in three ways. First, it confirms that thermonuclear design remained an active object of statutory secrecy in 1979. Second, it shows that the boundary between public derivation and classified synthesis could itself be contested. Third, it warns against assuming that an open publication record maps the full intellectual content of nuclear work.

**Legal context**

The direct evidence of classification stands on its own: the Intelligence Community symposium, Waterman’s account of classified national security reports, and the classified basis of the NSA medal. The Progressive establishes what the statutory regime demanded of a cleared scientist in exactly those years, and what the government was prepared to do to enforce it.


## Alternative explanations and counterarguments


### The open literature and what is not in it

The strongest objection to a claim of classified continuation is that the subject matter was published openly and at length, and that Los Alamos hosted and paid for much of it. Cellular automata, Monte Carlo, solitons, reversible computing, artificial life and complexity science all have substantial public literatures in exactly the relevant years, and the founding meetings of two of those fields were held at the laboratory. If this work was held behind classification, the shelf should be thin. It is not. The objection deserves a direct answer and the record supplies two.

The first is that Rota is not on the shelf. The founding conference of cellular automata as a field was held at Los Alamos from 7 to 11 March 1983, and its own preface records who paid for it: “Funds for the workshop were provided by the Theoretical Division, the Computing Division and the Center for Nonlinear Studies,” with the Center’s staff organizing. The proceedings are Physica D volume 10, twenty one papers. The direction of the money is worth stating plainly: three divisions of the laboratory paid to bring the outside world’s work on cellular automata onto the Los Alamos site for a week and have it presented there, and again in 1985 for evolution, games and learning. The laboratory convened the open version of these subjects at its own expense and got a first look at all of it. Rota was in his second decade as a consultant to that laboratory, and he is not among the authors and not in the acknowledgements. The contributors came mostly from outside — Wolfram from the Institute for Advanced Study, Toffoli, Margolus and Vichniac from MIT, Langton from Michigan, Kauffman from Pennsylvania, Hillis from Thinking Machines; the laboratory’s own were Doyne Farmer of the Center for Nonlinear Studies and Christian Burks of the Theoretical Biology and Biophysics group, with Crutchfield there as a long term Center visitor. Physica D volume 22 is the proceedings of the Center for Nonlinear Studies conference of May 1985, with Lapedes and Doolen of the Theoretical Division among the contributors. Rota’s three page memorial for Ulam opens that volume, every technical paper in it is by someone else, and across the entire run of the journal it is his only item. In Los Alamos Science number 15, the Ulam special issue, Rota writes The Lost Cafe and appears in transcribed conversations while Metropolis writes The Beginning of the Monte Carlo Method. He has no paper in the Artificial Life proceedings and no technical paper in the Monte Carlo methodology literature. In the nearest adjacent literature, the MIT work on reversible and physics-like computation, he is neither an author of nor named in Fredkin and Toffoli’s Conservative Logic, and he is neither author of nor thanked in Wolfram’s cellular automata papers of 1983 to 1986.

Santa Fe sharpens this rather than softening it. Rota’s own curriculum vitae lists, under administrative positions and not under research, “Trustee, The Santa Fe Institute, 1985–1990.” The Institute’s 1988 annual report to the Department of Energy confirms it: he is on the Board of Trustees, and he is not on the Science Board, where Metropolis and Wolfram both sit. He edits none of the SFI Studies in the Sciences of Complexity volumes and authors no working paper. For five years he sat on the board of the institution founded to publish this material in the open and published none of it there. His entire footprint across the whole corpus is a memorial, a memoir, the co-editorship of Ulam’s collected works, a trusteeship, and one page book notices in the journal he edited.

The second answer is that the synthesis is not in it either. Von Neumann published none of the automata theory himself. The Hixon lecture of September 1948 appeared in 1951 in a symposium volume and contains no cellular automaton. The five Illinois lectures of December 1949 he never wrote up; the tape made to help him do so, in Burks’s words, “turned out badly, with gaps in the text, unintelligible passages, and missing words,” von Neumann never edited the typescript, and “the recording itself is not extant.” Burks reconstructed Part I from that defective typescript and two sets of student notes, marking his own words in brackets and warning that much of the unbracketed text is heavily edited. Part II, the twenty nine state cellular model, was a first draft begun in 1952 and abandoned about a year later; Burks corrected its errors, named its organs, supplied all but twenty of its figures, and wrote a chapter of his own to finish the construction. It was published in 1966, nine years after the author’s death. What the open literature holds of von Neumann’s automata theory is an editor’s completion of an unfinished draft.

Nor does anyone in that literature claim the discipline. Langton’s definitional essay ends by asking that artificial life cease to differ in any fundamental way from biology. Bedau assigns the field two roots, von Neumann and Wiener, and describes a methodology rather than a union of sciences. Melanie Mitchell, writing from inside the field, states that “neither a single science of complexity nor a single complexity theory exists yet,” and traces its ancestry to cybernetics and systems science rather than to an unfinished von Neumann programme. Gell-Mann did propose a name for precisely the unification Rota called for, plectics, in 1995; he does not mention von Neumann in making the case, and the name did not take. A historian of the episode titles his study for the failed institutionalization of complexity science. Forty one years after Rota said the new unit remained to be named, it remains unnamed, and no existing field takes all five of theoretical computer science, neurophysiology, molecular biology, psychology and the mathematical theory of information under one name.

The same is true of the theorem. The axiomatization literature characterizes the functional and stops there: Shannon’s Appendix 2, Khinchin’s four axioms, Faddeev, and Aczél, Forte and Ng all establish that a short list of conditions forces the Shannon form, and none of them derives a physical distribution. Jaynes does not derive the distributions from an axiomatization either; he imports Shannon’s theorem by citation and obtains distributions by maximizing entropy under constraints, so that they arrive as variational solutions. Part I of his 1957 paper does not mention Fermi Dirac or Bose Einstein at all, and where those two enter the maximum entropy tradition later they come from the assumed state counting rather than from the entropy. Niven’s exact combinatorial derivation of 2005 does reach all three, but as three distinct entropies that approach Shannon like form only asymptotically. Hanel and Thurner’s axiomatic classification of entropies together with their distribution functions yields Boltzmann, power law and stretched exponential forms, and not Fermi Dirac or Bose Einstein. The literature thus divides into axiomatizations that stop at the functional and derivations that assume it. Rota’s chapter does both in sequence — the uniqueness of the functional, and the distributions as its maximisers — and it is in neither branch of the published record. The sources searched are listed in the notes.

That is what the open shelf contains and what it does not. It contains the public halves of both routes, written by everyone in the circle except the man this report concerns. It does not contain von Neumann’s own statement of his theory, a named science answering the description Ulam and Rota both gave, or the theorem Rota taught for years at MIT and never submitted.

### Composite verdict

Two claims were put forward for argument. On the first, a reasonable jury applying the more probable than not standard would find that von Neumann directed the trajectory of classified United States research toward the new science he and Ulam described, that the program ran on through discrete stochastic simulation, automata, nonlinear dynamics and artificial intelligence as an alternative to the continuous field and quantum simulation route that ran through open academic physics, and that the details of it were held inside the group that had done the Super work, with Rota in an authorized organizing and interpretive role at its center. On the second, the same jury would find that the entropy material of Chapter VIII — the uniqueness of the functional, and Rota’s statement that the distributions of statistical thermodynamics and quantum mechanics are determined by maximum entropy — taught, circulated, and never submitted, is central to that science. The formal title of the program and the text of Rota’s instructions are not in the public record. That is what the security system documented here was built to accomplish.


## Conclusion

Los Alamos converted the Super from a theoretical aspiration into a computational research program. ENIAC’s first large scale run attacked thermonuclear feasibility. Monte Carlo made neutron diffusion and multiplication tractable by sampling quantum physical histories on classical digital machines. MANIAC extended that practice into nonlinear dynamics and cellular automata. The foundational problem persisted: how can finite machines calculate, reproduce, and ultimately understand physical behavior whose possibilities grow beyond direct enumeration?

Ulam and Metropolis pursued that problem through classical discrete rules, probability, Boolean structure and emergent computation, on von Neumann’s premise that the continuous formalism was itself the thing to be replaced, and without separating the physics from the information or the machine from the organism. The public world divided what they had joined. Feynman granted the quantum formalism and proposed a simulator that uses quantum mechanics itself; Shannon made information a quantity indifferent to its carrier. Those two branches were built out, into quantum computing and into artificial intelligence, and they do not meet. Feynman’s question about what it means to understand the gods’ game nevertheless arrives at Ulam and Rota’s barrier of meaning from the opposite side: rules and complete physical description do not by themselves disclose the significance of a move or the contextual function of a key.

Rota became the program’s hermeneut and organizer. He gathered private lectures, connected researchers, edited Ulam and Metropolis, collaborated on binary arithmetic and complexity, organized the relevant Los Alamos conferences, entered high level laboratory politics, represented the Director’s Office in classified AI, wrote classified national security reports, and received classified NSA recognition. All of it sits inside the legal environment of Restricted Data and controlled scientific publication set out above. What is visible is the public surface of that activity; what would describe it directly is classified.

### Research limits

The text of Rota’s 1983 symposium remarks, his classified reports, the records underlying the NSA medal, and complete Director’s Office files remain unavailable. Those are precisely the records expected to identify his assignments directly. The role is therefore inferred from the cumulative visible conduct, and details such as the program’s formal title, internal chain of command, and individual classified deliverables remain unknown.
