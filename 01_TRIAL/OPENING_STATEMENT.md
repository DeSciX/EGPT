# Two Roads Out of Los Alamos

*Executive summary, in the form of an opening statement. What the undisputed facts are, what is actually in dispute, and what the evidence shows.*


## The case

This case is about a science that three men, John von Neumann, Stanislaw Ulam, and Gian-Carlo Rota said they were building and which they explicitly said was counter to the Bohr-Godel orthodoxy of physics and mathematics. It is a science that no journal has ever published, and that has no name.

It is also about a fork. After 1945 the public scientific world divided computation into two subjects and pursued them apart. One road runs from Claude Shannon: information as a measurable quantity indifferent to whatever carries it — coding, compression, statistical learning, and in the end the large language model. The other runs from Richard Feynman’s keynote at MIT in May 1981: accept quantum mechanics as it stands, build hardware that obeys it — quantum computing. Both roads are public, published and funded. They are travelled by different people in different departments who read different journals.

John von Neumann and Stanislaw Ulam did not see two subjects. They saw one. That version never reached a journal, and the evidence will show where it went instead.

The standard here is the ordinary civil one, more probable than not, and the question is what a reasonable person concludes from the whole pattern rather than from any single item in it.

## What is actually in dispute

Almost nothing above is contested. Four claims are, and they are the only four this report argues.

First, that von Neumann considered this new science which we would now call AI to be of utmost strategic importance to the US military, directed the trajectory of classified United States research toward that science, and that the details of it stayed inside the group that had done the Super work.

Second, that Ulam continued the work on the new AI science at Los Alamos, as did Rota after him, and that the entropy material Rota taught for twenty years and never submitted — the uniqueness of the entropy function, and the distributions of physics as determined by it — is central to that science.

Third, that these three men turned on the establishment of their own fields and took Einstein's side against it; that the route they took is the one the government followed while academic science took the public roads and not theirs; and that the Atomic Energy Act of 1954 is why they never said so in print.

Fourth, that von Neumann and Einstein were not two eminent men with private reservations but two men proposing to replace the foundations of their subjects — von Neumann for mathematics and for the quantum formalism he had himself written, Einstein for the field concept modern physics rests on — against the settled positions of Gödel and Bohr; that "The Mathematician" is an effective manifesto, as were Einstein's letters to Born and Besso; that the contempt each wrote with is the measure of how far he had gone; and that each was understood to be on the other's side of that question.

Each numbered fact below is marked with the charge it goes to. Charge One rests on One, Four, Five, Six, Seven, Eight and Fourteen, and most directly on Shannon's sentence at Six. Charge Two rests on Seven, Nine, Ten, Eleven and Twelve, and most directly on Rota's own curriculum vitae at Nine. Charge Three rests on Two, Three, Ten, Eleven, Twelve, Thirteen and Fourteen. For the turn against the establishment it rests most directly on three sentences: Ulam’s memorial sentence at Three, which places von Neumann with Einstein against Bohr and Gödel; Rota’s record, also at Three, of Ulam proposing to rebuild the CGS system — length, mass and time — out of a random walk; and the abstract at Ten in which Metropolis and Rota tell a refereed journal that the notion of computational complexity may have to be reinterpreted. For the silence that followed it rests on the bibliography at Eleven. Charge Four rests on Two and Three, and most directly on what von Neumann and Einstein wrote over their own names.

Everything else — the offices, the clearances, the appointments, the conference programmes, the editorial control, the publication counts, the award citations, and the statements the participants made over their own names — is evidence, placed before the reader to weigh.

## What the evidence shows

One man held the science, the machines and the authority, and said in public which way he thought physics and mathematics had to go. He died with the science unwritten, and the official record says a part of it existed only in his colleagues’ memories. The authority passed out of science. The machines on the east coast were switched off within eighteen months. And at the laboratory where the work had actually been done, in that same year, two new posts were created with oversight of research across every division, and one went to the man who had given von Neumann the cellular framework; the man who had built the machines carried them to Chicago and back; and a mathematician with a Q clearance, an editorship of the journals where new mathematics arrives for judgment, and later a National Security Agency compartment clearance, joined them and published, for the remaining twenty one years of his life, not one paper in the one subject they shared.

Meanwhile the public world built out both of its roads. Feynman’s became quantum computing. Shannon’s became artificial intelligence, and this document is being read on the second road taken to scale — a machine trained on a corpus that holds the open half of a divided record and treats the division as the natural order of the subject.

The reader is asked to do what a juror does: take the facts in the order they occurred, and decide whether the pattern is more probably the result of an authorized and continuing programme, or of coincidence.

This statement is not evidence. It is the case as this side puts it, and every fact in it is drawn from the two exhibits. Exhibit A is the report — Los Alamos, Computation, Artificial Intelligence and Gian-Carlo Rota — which carries the sources, the quotations, the year by year publication exhibit, the bibliography and the notes. Exhibit B gathers the record's own words for the premises of the twelve readings below, and for the premises of the third and fourth charges, with the location of each.

## The facts not in dispute

Begin with what no one contests, because most of this case is made of it.

One. By the end of his life von Neumann was not a scientist the government consulted. He set the direction of its highest priority research. From October 1954 he was a Commissioner of the Atomic Energy Commission, one of five men with statutory authority over the American nuclear enterprise and its Restricted Data. He chaired the committee whose report launched the intercontinental ballistic missile programme. At the 258th meeting of the National Security Council the Vice President asked him whether the missile directive went far enough and his amendment was adopted at the table. He held standing advisory positions across the weapons, missile and computing programmes for seventeen years, and received the Medal of Freedom and the first Fermi Award. When he was dying at Walter Reed, Life reported the Secretary of Defense and the Secretary of the Air Force at his bedside, and the Air Force’s own history records a Scientific Advisory Committee meeting convened at the hospital so the work could continue. And what he was working on in that room, with the service secretaries coming to him, was The Computer and the Brain — the Silliman Lectures he never delivered, a specification for a stochastic machine drawn from the nervous system, which his wife records went with him to the hospital and which he did not finish.

**Charge One.** The offices are the mechanism: a man holding them does not advise a research programme, he sets its direction. The work he was doing as he died names the subject he was setting it toward.

Two. He had said in print that the foundations of mathematics were unsound, and he had said it to the one man whose authority on the subject is now taken as final. “The Mathematician,” 1947, is not a survey of a field. It is a manifesto, published for a general readership, and this is the sentence at the centre of it: “The main hope of a justification of classical mathematics — in the sense of Hilbert or of Brouwer and Weyl — being gone, most mathematicians decided to use that system anyway. After all, classical mathematics was producing results which were both elegant and useful, and, even though one could never again be absolutely certain of its reliability, it stood on at least as sound a foundation as, for example, the existence of the electron.” That is the foundation of mathematics measured against the existence of the electron and found no better — written by the man who had given quantum mechanics its mathematical form, so he knew precisely what he was saying about each. The rigorous alternative existed and he priced it exactly: Brouwer’s intuitionism removed the contradictions, but “a good fifty per cent of modern mathematics, in its most vital — and up to then unquestioned — parts, especially in analysis, were also affected by this ‘purge’: they either became invalid or had to be justified by very complicated subsidiary considerations.” Half the subject, gone or salvaged at a cost. On Hilbert’s programme: “Gödel has shown that Hilbert’s program is essentially hopeless.”

And on 12 January 1931, writing from Berlin to Kurt Gödel himself — whose incompleteness theorems are today taught as the settled boundary of formal reasoning — von Neumann was blunt: “I absolutely disagree with your view on the formalizability of intuitionism. Certainly, for every formal system there is, as you proved, another formal one that is (already in arithmetic and the lower functional calculus) stronger. But intuitionism is not affected by that at all.” He pressed it: “is it not a fact, that not a single construction of the kind mentioned is known that cannot be formalized in A, and that no living logician is in the position of naming such [a construction]?” This is not a man on the margins of the foundations question. It is the man who first understood Gödel’s result telling its author, in writing, that he had drawn the line in the wrong place.

**Charge Four, and Charge Three.** This is the manifesto in mathematics: the foundation called no sounder than the electron, the replacement priced at half the subject, and the objection put in writing to the one authority the field now treats as final.

Three. He had heard the alternatives. On 23 and 24 March 1950, at the Seventh Conference on Cybernetics, Claude Shannon presented “The Redundancy of English.” Shannon attended as a guest; von Neumann is listed among the members, without the absence mark five others carry that year. At that same meeting, in the discussion of Ralph Gerard’s paper on digital notions in the central nervous system, von Neumann said the underlying mechanism of the nervous system “may be best, although somewhat loosely, described as an analogical mechanism,” and that as to whether its coded messages work like our digital system, “if I understand the evidence correctly, it is nonexistent in this regard.” He was in the room for information theory and did not take it as the account of the brain.

He held the same position in physics, and it was the minority one. In the nineteen fifties the Copenhagen interpretation of Bohr and Heisenberg was not one reading among several; it was the settled position of the discipline, and Einstein’s refusal of it was treated as the eccentricity of a great man past his best work. Ulam, in the official 1958 memorial, places von Neumann with Einstein in one sentence: “After the end of the war, he would express sentiments, somewhat similar to remarks reportedly made by Einstein, that the bewildering wealth of nuclear and elementary particle physics make premature any attempt to formulate a general quantum theory of fields, at least for the time being.” The man who wrote the mathematical foundations of quantum mechanics is recorded by his closest collaborator, in the official memorial, as standing on Einstein’s side of the question. That sentence is the connective tissue of this case. Einstein’s letters and von Neumann’s letters and essays are each a man speaking for himself; this is the witness who had the conversations saying they were speaking for the same position.

And the collaborator held the same position on his own account, and went further than refusing an interpretation. Ulam on complementarity, after a paragraph of warm praise for Bohr the man: “as a philosophical guideline, complementarity is essentially negative. It can only console.” Rota records what Ulam proposed in its place, in a sentence easy to read past: “One of the most striking is his proposal for the reconstruction of the CGS system on the basis of random walk.” The CGS system is centimetre, gram, second — length, mass and time, the three base units every physical quantity is built from. Ulam proposed to derive them from a random walk. That is not a disagreement about how to interpret a formalism. It is a proposal to rebuild the measuring system of physics itself out of a discrete stochastic process, and there is no more brazen way to turn on the standard account of what the physical world is made of.

**Charge Four, and Charge Three.** He was in the room for the alternatives in both subjects and refused them in both; his closest collaborator places him with Einstein — the position the discipline then treated as a great man's eccentricity — and that collaborator was himself proposing to rebuild length, mass and time from a random walk.

Four. He died on 8 February 1957, at fifty three, with none of the new science in print. The 1948 Hixon lecture contains no cellular automaton. The 1949 Illinois lectures he never wrote up. The automata manuscript was a first draft abandoned in 1953; Arthur Burks spent nine years correcting its errors, naming its parts, supplying most of its figures and writing a final chapter of his own before it could be published in 1966. The Computer and the Brain was unfinished on his desk.

**Charge One.** The science left almost no document, which is why what became of it has to be traced through people and institutions rather than through a bibliography.

Five. At his death the three things he personally held came apart, and each went somewhere the record can follow. The authority left science: his Commission seat stood vacant seven months and then went to John Stephens Graham, a lawyer. The machines were switched off: the Institute for Advanced Study computer project did not survive him by eighteen months, responsibility passed to Princeton on 1 July 1957, and at midnight on 15 July 1958 Julian Bigelow disconnected the power; the machine went to the Smithsonian in 1960 and the staff scattered to IBM, RAND, the Bureau of Standards and MIT. Freeman Dyson’s summary: “When von Neumann tragically died, the snobs took their revenge and got rid of the computing project root and branch.”

**Charge One.** The authority and the machines went where the record can follow them. The science did not.

Six — and this is the fact the rest of the case rests on. The science itself had never been written down. Claude Shannon, in the companion article to Ulam’s in the official 1958 memorial issue, on von Neumann’s automata work: “It is unfortunate that he was unable to complete the work he had in progress, some of which is in the form of rough notes or unedited lectures, and for some of which no record exists apart from his colleagues’ memories of casual conversations.” The founder of information theory, writing the official notice, states that part of the programme survived only in what the people around von Neumann remembered of conversation. Whatever continued after February 1957 continued through people. So the question is which people.

**Charge One.** This is the sentence the charge turns on, and it is the founder of information theory writing the official notice.

Seven. Los Alamos answered it in the same year, and Ulam wrote the answer down himself: “Returning to Los Alamos in 1957 after a year’s leave of absence at MIT, I was asked by Bradbury to accept one of the two newly created positions of research advisor to the director of the laboratory … Administratively, research advisors were to be on the same level as division leaders, and their duties were to oversee the research activities throughout the laboratory, in the various divisions … I held this position until 1967.” The year von Neumann died, the Director created two posts carrying research oversight across every division at division leader rank, and gave one of them to the man who had proposed the cellular framework in which von Neumann built his automaton. Ulam adds what changed for him: “A few years before Johnny’s death and increasingly so after … I became drawn into a maze of involvements. These had to do precisely with government science.” He titled that chapter Government Science and dated it 1957 to 1967. Hold on to the closing date. Metropolis came back to Los Alamos with the machines in 1965; Rota's own curriculum vitae dates his consultancy from 1966. So Ulam held laboratory-wide research oversight for two years after the machines returned and for one year after Rota arrived, and in that year all three were at the laboratory at once. The run of computation papers Rota wrote with Metropolis begins seven years later and runs 1973 to 1978.

**Charges One and Two.** The institution acted in the year of the death, creating the oversight rather than inheriting it, and gave it to the man who had supplied von Neumann's cellular framework.

Eight. The machines went with Nicholas Metropolis and came back. He had been at Los Alamos since April 1943, led the group that built MANIAC I and MANIAC II, named the Monte Carlo method, and is the Metropolis of the 1953 algorithm. Eight months after von Neumann died he left for Chicago — “I arrived in Chicago when Sputnik was —— October 4, 1957 is the date of Sputnik” — to found its Institute for Computer Research, where he designed MANIAC III, whose novel feature was unnormalised significance arithmetic. He returned to Los Alamos in 1965.

**Charge One.** The machines and the man who built them left and came back, which is what puts them in the same place, at the same time, as Rota in 1966.

Nine. Gian-Carlo Rota arrives in 1966, and his own curriculum vitae states the access under its own headings. Consultant: “Los Alamos Scientific Laboratory, 1966–.” Honors: “Director’s Office Fellow, Los Alamos National Laboratory, 1971–.” Security Clearances — a heading he saw no reason to leave out of a public document — “Top Secret Clearance (Air Force) 1969–1971. Q – Clearance (DOE), 1966–. SI – Clearance (NSA), 1981–.” He spoke at the United States Intelligence Community artificial intelligence symposium held at CIA headquarters in December 1983, as a Los Alamos Director’s Office Fellow, in the second day session block two of whose sessions carry the marking “THIS SESSION WILL BE CLASSIFIED SECRET”; wrote classified national security reports, and received the National Security Agency Medal for Distinguished Service in 1992 on a basis that remains classified.

**Charge Two.** Authorized, continuing, and on the subject: three clearances from three separate agencies across twenty-six years, a Director's Office fellowship, and the laboratory's own title carried into a classified artificial-intelligence forum.

Ten. He also held the gate. Founding editor of the Journal of Combinatorial Theory in 1966, editor of Advances in Mathematics from 1967 until his death on terms his publisher records as “complete editorial license,” founding editor of Advances in Applied Mathematics, first volume 1980 (immediately after the 1979 US vs The Progressive Case), editor of the Encyclopedia of Mathematics and Its Applications from its first volume, the series later moving to Cambridge University Press. As editor, Rota was in a position to see a broad swath of the industry advances, and which would get published and which wouldn't, while he remained under the continuing employ of government. And set against that gate, his own last published words on the subject. The 1977 Boolean analysis he wrote with Metropolis, Faltin and Ross closes its abstract like this: “The examination of carry propagation which arises in the Boolean analysis of functions may lead to a new interpretation of the notion of computational complexity.” A new interpretation of the notion of computational complexity. Computational complexity is the foundation of the theory of computation as the academic world had settled it, and two men at the centre of the laboratory’s computing are saying in a refereed journal that it is due to be reinterpreted — and that their own analysis of carry propagation is the thing that may do it. The two papers that follow, in 1978, are on the combinatorial structure of the faces of the n cube. After them the subject never appears in his technical literature again. Those are the last words Rota published on it. Between forty two and forty nine doctoral students at MIT, whom he placed — of one, in his own words: “Go to Los Alamos and look up Jim Louck.”

**Charges Two and Three.** Organizing and coordinating: he decided where new mathematics arrived for judgment, and he placed people into the laboratory by name. And the last thing he published on computation says the field's foundational notion is due to be reinterpreted.

Eleven. What he published can be counted, and it has been. A year by year exhibit of 189 located publications from 1956 to 1999, classified under a criterion fixed in advance by researchers given the criterion and not the argument, shows that Rota’s technical output never declined: fifty six technical items through 1978, fifty nine from 1979 to 1999, with his heaviest technical years at the very end of his life. What disappeared was a single subject. Six of his papers are on computation — 1973, 1974, 1975, 1977 and two in 1978 — and every one of the six was written with Metropolis. They run consecutively and stop. In the twenty one years that follow, across fifty nine technical papers including nine in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, there is not one paper on computation, machine architecture, simulation or quantum method.

**Charges Two and Three.** The output never declined; one subject left it, and only that one. That is the shape of a restriction, not of a career moving on.

Twelve. He taught what he did not publish, and what he taught was not what its title said. In Indiscrete Thoughts Rota sets down the relation between the two subjects as a slogan: “quantum mechanics is the imaginary square root of probability theory.” He does not leave it as a joke. The sentence after it is a programme: “A concept similar to that of a sample space should be brought into existence for amplitudes and quantum mechanics should be developed starting from this concept.” On that reading probability is the ground and quantum mechanics is built on it, and a course called Introduction to Probability is a course about the foundations of quantum mechanics. For decades Rota taught that MIT probability course from a manuscript of roughly four hundred pages, written with Kenneth Baclawski, photocopied and handed to students term after term. The earliest copy publicly available carries a 1979 copyright, and its table of contents settles when the material was in place: Chapter VII, “Entropy and Information,” with “Uniqueness of Entropy” at 7.14 and “The Shannon Coding Theorem” at 7.17. The same chapter is numbered VIII in the later MIT edition, the one this report quotes. So the uniqueness theorem and the coding theorem were both being handed to students in the year of The Progressive, and were still unsubmitted twenty years later when he died. The chapter states at the head what it will prove: “essentially all the interesting distributions we have seen in probability theory may be defined by entropy considerations.” It closes the section by naming the reach: “there are more distributions determined by maximum entropy, especially those in statistical thermodynamics and quantum mechanics.” And it proves a uniqueness theorem — five self-evident properties, and “if H satisfies the above five properties, then H is given by the formula introduced earlier in this chapter, except for a possible scale change.” Accept the five properties and exactly one entropy formula exists. The same chapter reaches the Shannon Coding Theorem, the founding theorem of information theory, as “a dramatic application of the law of large numbers.” It was submitted nowhere. Few mathematicians of the century had an easier path into a peer reviewed journal; he edited two of them and a Cambridge series. Not one proof in those four hundred pages took it.

**Charges Two and Three.** He taught the material for twenty years and submitted none of it, while holding the gate he could have walked through at will.

Thirteen. The open shelf is full, and he is not on it. The founding conference of cellular automata was held at Los Alamos in March 1983, funded, its own preface records, by “the Theoretical Division, the Computing Division and the Center for Nonlinear Studies” — three divisions of the laboratory paying to bring the outside world’s work in that subject onto its own site and have it presented there, and again in 1985. Rota, then in his eighteenth year as a consultant to that laboratory and its Director’s Office Fellow, is not among the twenty one authors and not in the acknowledgements. Across the entire run of the journal that published those proceedings he has one item, a three page memorial for Ulam. His own CV lists “Trustee, The Santa Fe Institute, 1985–1990” under administrative positions; the Institute’s 1988 report to the Department of Energy has him on the Board of Trustees and not on the Science Board, where Metropolis and Wolfram both sat. Five years on the board of the institution founded to publish this material openly, publishing none of it there.

**Charge Three.** The laboratory paid to convene this subject in the open, twice, and its own Director's Office Fellow is not in it.

Fourteen. And the science still has no name. Ulam said in the official memorial that von Neumann was engaged in “the creation of a possible new mathematical discipline … a combinatorial theory of automata and organisms.” Ulam wrote a book about that world and Rota edited it, and its table of contents is the new science laid out chapter by chapter: Science, Computers, and People: From the Tree of Mathematics, with “Experiments in Chess on Electronic Computing Machines,” “Patterns of Growth of Figures” and “More on Patterns of Growth” for the cellular automata Ulam had proposed to von Neumann, “Thermonuclear Devices,” “John von Neumann, 1903–1957” — the memorial itself — “Von Neumann: The Interaction of Mathematics and Computing,” and “John von Neumann on Computers and the Brain,” Ulam on the unfinished book. Rota edited the volume and opened it with his own address at Ulam's memorial service. Metropolis brought out the volume on the same material, A History of Computing in the Twentieth Century, which he and Rota edited together. Rota said in 1985 that “a new unit in science is being formed that remains to be named,” combining theoretical computer science, neurophysiology, molecular biology, psychology and the mathematical theory of information, and that “we need to name it glamorously.” Forty one years later no named discipline matches that description. The one attempt to name the union — Gell-Mann’s plectics, 1995 — does not mention von Neumann and did not take. Melanie Mitchell, from inside complexity science: “neither a single science of complexity nor a single complexity theory exists yet.” There is not one peer reviewed paper, by any of them, saying what the new science is.

**Charges One and Three.** Forty-one years on, the discipline three men described has no name and no paper — which is the condition the rest of this case sets out to explain.


## What we say the evidence means

Twelve readings follow. They are this side's, not findings, and a reader is free to reject any of them — but each is a reading an ordinary person takes from these documents without being led to it, and the sources for every one are gathered reading by reading in Exhibit B.

**(i) The offices and the dying work, together.** Von Neumann held the highest, and perhaps unprecedented, set of roles in directing military technology — Commissioner of the Atomic Energy Commission, chairman of the ICBM committee — and at the time of his meeting with the Joint Chiefs, as he was dying, he was working feverishly on *The Computer and the Brain*. An ordinary reader takes the implication: that he was talking to them about this science as a strategic technology, one that potentially exceeded the nuclear capability they had already mastered.

**(ii) The obituary, written by his best friend.** Ulam and von Neumann were the closest of friends, and it is Ulam who writes the official memorial. In it he coins the Singularity; he says von Neumann was only making a start on a new science, in a career like that one; and he places him pointedly with Einstein. A reader who knows that career finds “only a beginning” shocking, not modest.

**(iii) The same rite, one generation apart.** Ulam named Rota his most eminent “influencee.” Rota gave Ulam's memorial at Los Alamos as the opening address of the laboratory's own conference, and the address was entirely about seeing the future and about AI. The foreseen future and AI are the subject of von Neumann's obituary, written by his best friend, and of Ulam's memorial, given by his self-proclaimed influencee. That is not a coincidence a reader passes over.

**(iv) The hermeneut chain.** Ulam took up part of von Neumann's duties after his death, as an administrative head with oversight across the laboratory. Ulam said von Neumann's dying work was on what we would now call AI and that it ranked with his most fertile ideas. Rota described his own role as the hermeneut hired to render Ulam's cryptic messages into mathematics. And Rota's memorial for Ulam was a last translation of the prophet, on AI and the barrier of meaning.

**(v) The tone of the letters.** To Born, Einstein calls the theory inconsistent with a physics representing “a reality in space and time without phantom actions over distances”; says the objects connected by laws will prove to be “not probabilities, but conceived facts, as one took for granted only a short time ago”; and closes by calling on “my little finger as a witness, which cannot claim any authority to be respected outside my own skin.” Earlier he records that “our younger colleagues interpret this as a consequence of senility.” To Besso: if physics cannot rest on the field concept, “nothing remains of my entire castle in the air, gravitation theory included, [and of] the rest of modern physics.” That is biting sarcasm from the man whose name is a synonym for genius, and the last of it is jaw-dropping, because one half of modern physics was ushered in by the work that won him the Nobel Prize and the other half by his general relativity, and he is saying both may go. And the other great genius of the century was attacking the same establishment in the same years, in the same register.

**(vi) The year the subject stops.** Rota's last published paper on computation is dated 1978. In the twenty-one years that follow he publishes fifty-nine further technical papers and not one on computation, while editing two journals and a Cambridge series, while continuing to publish with Metropolis until 1995, and while editing the computer books of both Metropolis and Ulam. Only his own work on the subject stops. The following year, 1979, is the year of *United States v. The Progressive* and the year of the earliest surviving printing of the probability manuscript.

**(vii) What the other founders said, and when.** Shannon told the same conference at which von Neumann entered his caveat on the nervous system that “we are not at all interested in semantics or the meaning implications of information,” and in 1958 wrote that part of von Neumann's last work survived only in “his colleagues' memories of casual conversations.” Feynman, who launched quantum computing, supposed “that quantum mechanics is correct” and then drew the boundary himself: “Discretizing is the right word. Quantizing is a different kind of mathematics.” Feynman was the one significant member of the Super group who left.

**(viii) How Rota spoke of his own profession.** In “A Mathematician's Gossip” he writes of “the handwaving arguments with which mathematically illiterate physicists have been pestering us”; of physicists who make “a curious distinction between ‘proofs’ and ‘rigorous proofs,’ falling prey to a *folie* of mystical trance in which they can make themselves believe anything”; and of a field then in fashion whose “self-styled specialists” do not know mathematics. Elsewhere in the same book he calls the portrait of the scientist as “free from emotion, from pettiness, from the passions of the world” a “heritage of lies.” A man who held the gate of two journals published that about the establishment he belonged to.

**(ix) EPR is a field-theory judgement.** You cannot have the EPR question without one: whether quantum mechanics is complete is a question about what the underlying objects are. Einstein wrote that physics may not rest “on the field concept, i.e., on continuous structures,” and that one may have to “avoid the continuum (together with space and time) altogether.” Von Neumann asked Dirac for operators with “discrete (point) spectra, in order to avoid the difficulties connected with the point electron,” told Schrödinger the correlations were “pseudo-problems,” and wrote that classical mathematics stood on a foundation no sounder than “the existence of the electron.” Ulam's report that von Neumann expressed sentiments “somewhat similar to remarks reportedly made by Einstein” about the prematurity of a general quantum field theory is not a separate matter from the completeness of quantum mechanics.

**(x) The claim, not the hedge.** The 1977 abstract closes: “The examination of carry propagation which arises in the Boolean analysis of functions may lead to a new interpretation of the notion of computational complexity.” A reader who stresses “may lead to” finds a project that never started. What the qualifier is attached to is four men telling a refereed journal that the notion of computational complexity is due to be reinterpreted and that their own work may be what does it — the foundation of the theory of computation as the academic world settled it. They are the last words Rota published on the subject.

**(xi) Modesty is not a competing claim.** Ulam calls the cellular model “a general model, considered by von Neumann and the author.” Burks, editing von Neumann's own manuscript, attributes the suggestion to Ulam. Metropolis describes the identical pattern for Monte Carlo: Ulam had the idea, “he discussed this idea with von Neumann,” and von Neumann took it up. Ulam was self-deprecating about origination all his life. To set his modest phrasing against his colleagues' attribution is to turn a man's manners into evidence against him.

**(xii) The witness who appointed him.** The fuller account of the scene at Walter Reed — the Deputy Secretary of Defense, the three service secretaries, the Joint Chiefs — comes from Lewis Strauss, Chairman of the Atomic Energy Commission, who had recommended von Neumann's appointment as Commissioner, rather than from the *Life* article. The Chairman who put him in the seat is among the best-placed witnesses alive to who came to that room. To discount him for the magazine he did not appear in is to substitute a question about venue for a question about knowledge.


## The three proposals are one proposal

Read the three refoundations back to back, because separately they look like three eminent men with three hobbies and together they are a programme.

Von Neumann, in the book he did not finish, on mathematics: “Just as languages like Greek or Sanskrit are historical facts and not absolute logical necessities, it is only reasonable to assume that logics and mathematics are similarly historical, accidental forms of expression.” Not discovered, not necessary — an accident. And the reason he thought so: “whatever language the central nervous system is using, it is characterized by less logical and arithmetical depth than what we are normally used to,” so that “when we talk mathematics, we may be discussing a secondary language, built on the primary language truly used by the central nervous system.”

Ulam, on physics: “his proposal for the reconstruction of the CGS system on the basis of random walk.” Length, mass and time, rebuilt from a stochastic process.

Rota, on quantum mechanics: “A concept similar to that of a sample space should be brought into existence for amplitudes and quantum mechanics should be developed starting from this concept.” And on computation, in the last thing he published on the subject: the analysis “may lead to a new interpretation of the notion of computational complexity.”

Mathematics, physics, computation. Three foundations, three men, and in every case the proposal is to tear the foundation out and put something else under it. And the something else is the same in all three: a discrete stochastic process, of the kind von Neumann says the nervous system runs on.

That is not an interpretation supplied by this document. It is what Ulam named in the official memorial — “a combinatorial theory of automata and organisms,” computation and living things in one breath — and what he gave as von Neumann's motive: “one of his motives for pressing for the development of electronic computers was his fascination with the working of the nervous system and the organization of the brain itself.” It is what Rota named again twenty seven years later, at the same laboratory, out of the same ingredients: “a new unit in science is being formed that remains to be named. It will include the best of theoretical computer science, neurophysiology, molecular biology, psychology, and the mathematical theory of information. It will be important to name it properly. As the Latin proverb says, ‘Nomen est omen.’” His interviewer translates it in the next line: “The name is a presage.”

It was never named. That is the whole of this case.


## Why the two public roads could not find it

Shannon’s theorem is about messages and is indifferent to what carries them. That indifference is what made it portable, and it is why the branch could run seventy years to the language model without asking a question about physics. Feynman’s simulator is about physics and treats information as what you read out at the end. Neither asks whether the entropy of a physical system and the entropy of a message are the same object or merely similar ones.

Von Neumann and Ulam never divided them, and the evidence is in what they built. The Computer and the Brain is not a comparison of a machine to an organ but the specification of a machine drawn from the organ, stochastic and deliberately imprecise in arithmetic. Ulam states the motive outright: “one of his motives for pressing for the development of electronic computers was his fascination with the working of the nervous system and the organization of the brain itself.” The automata theory is a theory of computation and of living things in one breath. Monte Carlo appears in Ulam’s own book as the general method for problems intractable at any scale, from quantum statistics through the replication of a living cell to cosmology.

Which is what makes that chapter the centre of this case rather than a footnote to it. The distributions of statistical thermodynamics and quantum mechanics are determined by maximum entropy, and there is exactly one entropy. Both statements are Rota’s, in the same chapter. The entropy of physics and the entropy of information are therefore not two quantities that share an algebraic form. The published literature divides along the same seam as the world: the axiomatic tradition after Shannon fixes the functional and derives no physical distribution; Jaynes and maximum entropy assume the functional and obtain distributions by maximising it. Rota taught both halves in one chapter and submitted neither.
